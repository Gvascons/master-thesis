\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:fundamentacao}

This chapter fixes the vocabulary the rest of the dissertation uses
--- metrics, model families, distillation, distributional regression,
statistical comparison and decision --- and then positions the work
in the literature, axis by axis, closing with a map of the gaps each
research question attacks. Depth follows use: what the later
chapters measure or test is defined here; what they do not use is
left to the extended technical report.

\section{Tabular learning and its metrics}
\label{sec:fund-tabular}
\label{sec:fund-metricas-clf}

A tabular dataset is a table in which each row is an observation and
each column an attribute. Heterogeneity is constitutive: a single
table mixes continuous variables on different scales, counts,
ordinal and nominal categories of arbitrary cardinality, and missing
values, and each column is an independent semantic axis --- swapping
two columns does not change the problem. Tables offer no universal
invariance (spatial, sequential) that an architecture could exploit
by construction, which is the usual explanation for why, for more
than a decade, the state of the art on tables belonged to ensembles
of decision trees \citep{grinsztajn2022}. Given a training set
$D = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$, the goal is a function that
predicts the target of \emph{new} observations well; every honest
evaluation therefore measures performance on data that neither the
model nor the model-selection process has seen
(Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}). This work considers binary and
multiclass classification and regression, and distinguishes two
readings of regression: the \emph{point} reading, in which the model
returns one number (an estimate of the conditional mean), and the
\emph{distributional} reading, in which it returns a full predictive
distribution, from which calibration is demanded
(Section~\ref{sec:fund-distribucional}).

Each task has one threshold-free primary metric. For binary
classification, the \emph{ROC-AUC} is the probability that a
randomly drawn positive receives a higher score than a randomly drawn
negative \citep{hanley1982}; chance scores 0.5, perfection 1, and
since it depends on neither threshold nor prevalence it compares
models rather than operating points \citep{fawcett2006}. For
multiclass, the \emph{log-loss}, $-\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \log \hat
p_i(y_i)$, is a proper scoring rule: its expectation is minimized by
reporting the true probability, so it punishes both errors and badly
calibrated confidence \citep{gneiting2007}. For regression, the
\emph{RMSE}, $\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}$, measures
point error in the unit of the target and penalizes large errors
quadratically (MAE and $R^2$ are secondary). Accuracy, by contrast,
is treacherous under class imbalance because it ignores how errors
split between classes: on \texttt{amazon\_employee}, where only 5.8\%
of requests are denied, XGBoost reaches 94.3\% hold-out accuracy
while detecting only 3.2\% of the requests that should have been
denied. A model that approved everything would have nearly identical
accuracy and zero utility; under imbalance, accuracy mostly measures
prevalence, not the model.

\section{Model families}
\label{sec:fund-modelos}

\subsection{Gradient-boosted decision trees}
\label{sec:fund-gbdt}

A decision tree predicts through a sequence of binary questions on
single attributes until it reaches a leaf \citep{cart1984}; its
partitions are axis-aligned and invariant to monotone
transformations of each attribute, so column normalization is
irrelevant to it. A single tree is either shallow and coarse (high
bias) or deep and unstable (high variance) \citep{hastie2009}.
\emph{Boosting} attacks the bias: it trains shallow trees
sequentially, each fitted to what the accumulated committee still
gets wrong, and predicts with their weighted sum. Formally, gradient
boosting is gradient descent in function space \citep{friedman2001}:
at round $m$ a tree $h_m$ is fitted to the negative gradient of the
loss at the current predictions (for squared error, the residuals
$y_i - F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x}_i)$), and $F_m = F_{m-1} + \nu\, h_m$ with
a small learning rate $\nu$. Because only the gradient of the loss
enters, the same GBDT can minimize squared error (a mean), the
pinball loss (a quantile, Section~\ref{sec:fund-distribucional}), or
several outputs at once --- which is what lets
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} use an XGBoost as a 19-quantile
student. The benchmark evaluates the three dominant implementations:
XGBoost \citep{chen2016} (regularized objective), LightGBM
\citep{ke2017} (leaf-wise growth with histograms) and CatBoost
\citep{prokhorenkova2018} (ordered target statistics and ordered
boosting). For a surprisingly high number of datasets, light tuning
of a GBDT matters more than the choice between neural networks and
GBDTs \citep{mcelfresh2023}; in this dissertation the
GBDTs play the role of the null hypothesis that every new proposal
must be measured against.

\subsection{Deep learning for tabular data}
\label{sec:fund-dl}

The \emph{multi-layer perceptron} stacks layers
$\mathbf{h}^{(l)} = \phi(\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l-1)} +
\mathbf{b}^{(l)})$ trained by backpropagation \citep{rumelhart1986};
on tables the question was never expressiveness but whether training
finds, with finite heterogeneous data, solutions as good as the
GBDTs'. One-hot encoding of categorical attributes explodes with
cardinality (the benchmark caps it at 50 categories per column,
Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}); the standard alternative is learned
embeddings, which the architectures below adopt in some form. Two recent
proposals show that much of the MLP's historical disadvantage was one
of recipe: \textbf{RealMLP} \citep{realmlp2024} competes with tuned
GBDTs through numeric embeddings, scheduling, regularization and
preprocessing, and \textbf{TabM} \citep{tabm2025} obtains the effect
of an ensemble of MLPs at close to the cost of one, through
sub-networks that share most of their weights.

The second lineage imports attention \citep{vaswani2017}, treating
the columns of a row as tokens of an encoder-only Transformer.
\textbf{TabNet} \citep{arik2021} combines attention with sparse,
learned attribute selection; the \textbf{FT-Transformer}
\citep{gorishniy2021} tokenizes each attribute and runs a standard
Transformer, and is the reference of the lineage; \textbf{SAINT}
\citep{somepalli2021} adds attention \emph{between rows} of the
batch; \textbf{STab} \citep{stab-ref} uses local stochastic
competition units and predicts by averaging 64 stochastic passes,
whose cost appears on the latency axis of
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}.

The third lineage, \emph{Kolmogorov--Arnold networks} (KAN)
\citep{kan2024}, inverts the MLP's design after the
Kolmogorov--Arnold representation theorem
\citep{kolmogorov1957,arnold1957}: learnable univariate functions
(B-splines) live on the edges and the nodes merely sum.
\textbf{TabKAN} \citep{tabkan2025} organizes variants with other
bases (Chebyshev polynomials, radial basis functions) into a
framework aimed at tabular data. Under parameter and FLOP parity the
MLP beats KAN on most machine-learning tasks \citep{kancritical2024};
the independent tabular evaluation of \citet{poeta2024} favors KAN
in accuracy, but without GBDT baselines and with ten-epoch training.

\subsection{Tabular foundation models and in-context learning}
\label{sec:fund-fm}

Every model above is trained anew for each dataset; tabular
foundation models (TFMs) abandon that premise. An \emph{in-context learning}
(ICL) model is a pre-trained Transformer that receives, in a single
input, the entire training set --- pairs $(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$ ---
followed by the query row, and produces the prediction in one
forward pass with frozen weights: ``training'' on the task reduces
to assembling the context, and all the learning happened during
pre-training (Figure~\ref{fig:icl-mecanismo}).

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics{images/tikz-cap2-2-en.pdf}
\caption[The in-context learning mechanism]{The in-context learning
(ICL) mechanism, in its two phases: synthetic pre-training and
prediction by a single forward pass.}
\label{fig:icl-mecanismo}
\end{figure}

The ability to infer without gradients comes from the prior.
\emph{Prior-fitted networks} (PFNs) are pre-trained on millions of
small synthetic datasets sampled from a prior over generating
mechanisms; on each, the network sees a labeled context and learns
to predict held-out labels, so its forward pass approximates the
\emph{posterior predictive distribution} under that prior, given the
context \citep{muller2022pfn}. In TabPFN, the prior is built from
structural causal models --- random causal graphs whose variables
relate through randomly drawn functions and noise terms
\citep{hollmann2025}; \citet{nagler2023} complements the Bayesian
reading with a frequentist analysis of PFNs as pre-tuned but
untrained predictors. \textbf{TabPFN}, in its v2
\citep{hollmann2025} and v2.5 \citep{priorlabs2025} versions,
established the paradigm in practice, with a native context limit
(50{,}000 rows in v2.5). In regression its output layer returns a
\emph{bar distribution} --- a discretized histogram over the
target's range from which arbitrary quantiles can be read --- so it
natively delivers distributional regression. \textbf{TabFM}
\citep{tabfm2026,kong2026} is an independent implementation of the
paradigm, with a hybrid architecture (attention between columns,
compression of each row into tokens, an ICL Transformer) and
separate checkpoints per task; in regression it decodes a single
scalar, so it is an exclusively \emph{point} teacher. This asymmetry
of output heads, not any difference in average accuracy, is what
makes TabPFN the teacher of Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}. Both
models carry limits inherited from pre-training: TabFM admits at
most 10 classes and was pre-trained with contexts of up to
16{,}384 rows \citep{kong2026} (larger tables depend on length generalization or
subsampling), and the 100-class \texttt{helena} dataset exceeds the
class limit of both models, which becomes two structural exclusions
in the benchmark.

The mechanism has a structural price: \emph{the training set travels
with the model on every prediction}. Training costs almost nothing,
but each inference pass reprocesses the whole context, putting the
cost per prediction orders of magnitude above a GBDT's, which walks a
few trees. In practice the model runs several times on perturbed
versions of the context (column permutations, preprocessing
variants) and averages the outputs --- an \emph{inference ensemble}
whose size multiplies the cost. Three levers cheapen inference
without leaving the paradigm: shrink the ensemble, shrink the context
by subsampling, or replace the model with a distilled student;
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} measures all three on the same
yardstick.

\section{Knowledge distillation}
\label{sec:fund-destilacao}

Knowledge distillation trains a fast model (the \emph{student}) to
imitate the outputs of an expensive one (the \emph{teacher}). It
originated as model compression \citep{bucila2006} and acquired its
modern form with \citet{hinton2015}, whose key observation is that
the probabilities a teacher assigns to the wrong classes carry
information the true label lacks; the student is trained against
these \emph{soft targets}, softened by a temperature, together with
the true labels. This is \emph{response-based} distillation: what is
transferred is the teacher's output, not its internals. In
regression the label is replaced by the teacher's predicted value
--- and, in the distributional extension of
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}, by the teacher's entire quantile
curve. The \emph{born-again} lineage shows that the pair is
agnostic: \citet{breiman1996} compress an ensemble of trees into a
single tree, \citet{vidal2020} give ensemble compression an exact
modern treatment, \citet{frosst2017} distill a network into a soft
decision tree, and in born-again networks of equal capacity even
self-imitation can improve the student \citep{furlanello2018}.

A distilled student is judged against a \emph{control}: the same
student architecture and configuration trained on the true labels,
so that the comparison measures what learning from the teacher's
outputs adds over learning from the data alone. With ICL teachers, distillation has an additional
attraction --- it removes the family's constitutive bottleneck, the
context that travels with every prediction. It also has a specific
hazard: a teacher that scores rows it holds in its own context
produces nearly deterministic labels, collapsing the soft targets.
The safeguard is \emph{out-of-fold} (OOF) labeling: the training
pool is split into folds and each fold is labeled by the teacher
conditioned only on the others, never on itself \citep{pocketfm2026}.
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} inherits this safeguard and shows, in
an ablation on four datasets, that in distributional regression what
it protects is calibration, not accuracy.

\hdrshort{Distributional regression}
\section{Distributional regression: quantiles, CRPS and calibration}
\sectionmark{Distributional regression}
\label{sec:fund-distribucional}

Two models with the same point error can differ radically in the
quality of the uncertainty they report, and point metrics are blind
to the difference. The conditional quantile $q_\tau(\mathbf{x})$ is
the value below which the target falls with probability $\tau$,
given $\mathbf{x}$: $\tau = 0.5$ is the conditional median, and
$q_{0.1}$ and $q_{0.9}$ delimit a central 80\% interval. Quantile
regression \citep{koenker1978} estimates $q_\tau$ by minimizing the
\emph{pinball loss}: with $u = y - \hat{q}_\tau$,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\tau(u) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \tau\, u, & u \geq 0,\\
    (\tau - 1)\, u, & u < 0,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:pinball}
\end{equation}
an asymmetric ramp whose expected-loss minimizer is exactly the
$\tau$ quantile (for $\tau=0.9$, underestimating costs 0.9 per unit
and overestimating 0.1). Training on a grid of levels
$\mathcal{T} = \{0.05, \ldots, 0.95\}$ yields a quantile curve, a
discretization of the whole predictive distribution; when the levels
are fitted independently the curve may cross
($\hat{q}_{0.8} < \hat{q}_{0.7}$), and re-sorting each row's
quantiles restores monotonicity without worsening the total pinball
loss.

Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} obtains quantile models in two ways.
The \emph{control}, with no teacher, is trained by pinball loss on
the observed targets, discovering each quantile from a single $y$
per row:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ctrl}} \;=\; \frac{1}{n\,|\mathcal{T}|}
  \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}}
  \rho_\tau\!\left(y_i - \hat{q}_\tau(\mathbf{x}_i)\right).
  \label{eq:perda-controle}
\end{equation}
The \emph{distilled student} receives the quantile ready-made: for
each row and level the teacher provides
$q^{\mathrm{teach}}_\tau(\mathbf{x}_i)$, and the student learns it
by ordinary multi-output regression with squared error:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dist}} \;=\; \frac{1}{n\,|\mathcal{T}|}
  \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}}
  \left(\hat{q}_\tau(\mathbf{x}_i) -
  q^{\mathrm{teach}}_\tau(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)^2.
  \label{eq:perda-destilado}
\end{equation}
The two arms therefore differ in the target \emph{and} in the loss.
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} mitigates this asymmetry on the
configuration side, re-tuning the hyperparameters of the two arms
separately; the effect of the loss difference itself is not
isolated.

\textbf{CRPS.} The yardstick for predictive distributions is the
continuous ranked probability score: for a predictive CDF $F$ and
observed $y$,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{CRPS}(F, y) \;=\;
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
  \left( F(z) - \mathbf{1}\{z \geq y\} \right)^2 \mathrm{d}z,
  \label{eq:crps}
\end{equation}
the integrated squared distance between the predicted CDF and the
step CDF of the observation. It is a proper scoring rule
\citep{gneiting2007} --- no score is gained by reporting a
distribution other than the one the model believes --- and it
generalizes the absolute error: if $F$ collapses to a point,
Eq.~\ref{eq:crps} reduces to $|\hat{y} - y|$, so point and
distributional models are compared on the same scale, in the unit of
the target; lower is better. From a quantile grid, the CRPS is
estimated as twice the mean pinball loss over the levels
\citep{gneiting2011},
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\operatorname{CRPS}}(F, y) \;=\;
  \frac{2}{|\mathcal{T}|} \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}}
  \rho_\tau\!\left(y - \hat{q}_\tau\right),
  \label{eq:crps-grade}
\end{equation}
which also makes explicit that minimizing the pinball loss over the
grid is, up to a constant, optimizing the discretized CRPS. The
estimator carries a grid-dependent bias, so its values support
internal comparisons, never absolute comparisons across works.

\textbf{Coverage and reliability.} A nominal 80\% interval is
calibrated if it covers the target in about 80\% of test cases; the
PICP (\emph{prediction interval coverage probability}) is that
fraction. A PICP far below nominal exposes an overconfident model
(intervals too narrow), far above an underconfident one (too wide).
Coverage is necessary but not sufficient --- an absurdly wide
interval always covers --- so it is read together with
\emph{sharpness}, the average interval width
\citep{gneiting2007calib}. The per-quantile analogue is the
\emph{reliability curve}: for each grid level $\tau$, the empirical
coverage $\hat P(y \leq q_\tau)$ is plotted against $\tau$, and a
calibrated model lies on the diagonal. The reading rule is per
level: a curve \emph{above} the diagonal at some $\tau$ means the
predicted quantile is too \emph{high} at that level, and below it,
too low. The coverage of a central interval
$[q_{0.10}, q_{0.90}]$ is the \emph{difference} between the fractions
at its two levels, so what decides it is the curve's \emph{slope}
between those levels, not its position: steeper than the diagonal
means intervals too wide (overcoverage), flatter means too narrow
(undercoverage), and there is no shortcut ``above the diagonal =
overcovers'' valid for the whole curve. An S shape around the
diagonal is the signature of badly calibrated tails with a correct
center. Figure~\ref{fig:quantis-cobertura} makes the mechanism
concrete with real data: the prediction for one row is a curve of 19
values in the unit of the target, and counting, over all rows, how
often the target falls below each level gives the empirical coverage
--- here an S shape, with coverage $8.2\%$ at nominal level $0.20$
(tails too wide, center nearly perfect). The levels $\tau$ are not
probabilities estimated by the model but grid levels fixed by the
evaluator; probability enters only in the verification. This is the
reading of Figure~\ref{fig:destil-calibration}.

Two further tools close the kit. Split-conformal calibration adjusts
the interval bounds by the empirical quantile of residuals on a
held-out calibration set, guaranteeing marginal coverage under
exchangeability \citep{vovk2005}, with a quantile variant (CQR)
\citep{romano2019}; Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} uses it as a
pilot. And two classical models produce predictive distributions
without any teacher: \emph{quantile regression forests} (QRF) keep
all training targets in each leaf and read quantiles from their
weighted empirical distribution \citep{meinshausen2006}, and
\emph{NGBoost} boosts the parameters of a parametric distribution by
natural gradients on a proper scoring rule \citep{duan2020}, a
member of the wider LSS family \citep{marz2019}. They are the
natural teacher-free baselines of Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}.

\section{Statistical comparison of algorithms}
\label{sec:fund-estatistica}

Metrics are not commensurable across datasets (an AUC of 0.95 on an
easy dataset and of 0.75 on a hard one are not on the same scale),
so the canonical framework of \citet{demsar2006} works with
\emph{ranks}: on each dataset the $k$ models are ordered from best
($1$) to worst ($k$). The unit of analysis is the \emph{dataset}:
folds of the same dataset share most of their examples, and treating
them as replicates would inflate the effective $N$
(pseudo-replication); each dataset contributes one number per model,
here the measurement on the fixed hold-out.

\textbf{Omnibus and post-hoc.} The Friedman test \citep{friedman1937}
asks whether the differences between the average ranks $R_j$ of $k$
models over $N$ datasets are explainable by chance; under the null,
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2_F \;=\; \frac{12N}{k(k+1)}
  \left[ \sum_{j=1}^{k} R_j^2 \;-\; \frac{k(k+1)^2}{4} \right]
  \label{eq:friedman}
\end{equation}
is approximately chi-squared with $k-1$ degrees of freedom. The
approximation is conservative at small $N$, so the Iman--Davenport
correction $F_F = (N-1)\chi^2_F / [N(k-1) - \chi^2_F]$, which follows
an $F$ distribution, is also reported \citep{imandavenport1980}. A
significant Friedman says only that \emph{some} difference exists.
The Nemenyi post-hoc \citep{nemenyi1963} declares two models
different if their average ranks are further apart than the
\emph{critical difference}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CD} \;=\; q_\alpha \sqrt{\frac{k(k+1)}{6N}},
  \label{eq:cd}
\end{equation}
with $q_\alpha$ from the studentized range distribution. In a CD
diagram, models are placed at their average rank and bars join
groups whose ranks are less than CD apart: models under the same bar
are not statistically distinguishable --- which does not mean they
are equal --- and, since CD grows with $k$ and shrinks slowly with
$N$, wide bars are the expected consequence of many models and few
datasets.

\textbf{Pairwise tests.} For specific pairs, the Wilcoxon
signed-rank test \citep{wilcoxon1945} is more powerful than Nemenyi
because it uses the magnitudes of the per-dataset differences; across
many pairs, Holm's step-down procedure controls the family-wise
error less conservatively than Bonferroni \citep{holm1979}. One
arithmetic limit matters here: with $N$ datasets the smallest
two-sided Wilcoxon p-value is $2 \cdot (1/2^N)$, so with $N = 5$ the
floor is $0.0625$ and rejecting at $0.05$ is impossible before any
correction. The \emph{sign test} \citep{conover1999} ignores
magnitudes and counts positive differences, exact under a
Binomial$(N, 1/2)$; it is fully robust to scale, which makes it the
natural test when differences live on incommensurable scales, as do
the retentions of Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}.

\textbf{Effect size and variance decomposition.} Significance does
not measure relevance. Cohen's paired effect size, $d_z = \bar{d} /
s_d$, expresses the mean per-dataset difference in units of its own
variability. The correlation ratio $\eta^2 =
\mathit{SS}_{\text{between}} / \mathit{SS}_{\text{total}}$, applied
to the ranks, splits the total variance into a part attributable to
the family, a part attributable to the model within the family, and
a residual which, with one observation per model$\times$dataset,
bundles the model$\times$dataset interaction and the noise,
inseparably. A high family share would say the family determines
performance; a high residual, that the winner changes from dataset
to dataset.

\textbf{Resampling and instance level.} The percentile bootstrap
\citep{efron1993} resamples with replacement $B$ times and takes the
2.5 and 97.5 percentiles of the recomputed statistic as a 95\%
interval; resampling datasets gives the uncertainty of a
cross-dataset average, resampling a dataset's test rows (paired, when
two models share them) that of a result on that dataset. McNemar's
test \citep{mcnemar1947} compares two classifiers on the same rows
using only the rows where they disagree --- the sign test applied to
instances rather than datasets. Spearman's rank correlation, used
for the stability of latency rankings across datasets, measures
whether two orderings agree (1 identical, $-1$ reversed).

\textbf{The Bayesian test with a region of practical equivalence.}
The frequentist framework can reject equality but cannot
\emph{establish} it; non-rejection with small $N$ is not evidence of
equivalence. \citet{benavoli2017} offer the Bayesian complement. A
\emph{region of practical equivalence} (ROPE) $[-r, r]$ is fixed by
domain judgment as the band within which a performance difference is
irrelevant (in this study the radius and the decision cutoffs were
fixed after the analysis began; Chapters~\ref{chap:metodologia}
and~\ref{chap:benchmark}). The $N$ paired differences are treated as
a sample from an unknown distribution with a non-parametric prior (a
Dirichlet process with a pseudo-observation at zero, of weight 0.6
in this study). The object of interest is
$\theta = (\theta_{\text{left}}, \theta_{\text{ROPE}},
\theta_{\text{right}})$, summing to 1: the probabilities that the
typical difference between the two models (formally, the mean of two
drawn differences) falls left of the ROPE, inside it, or right of
it. Its posterior is sampled, and two summaries coexist that do not
give the same number: the \emph{expected mass} in a region,
$E[\theta_{\text{ROPE}}]$, read in this study with a 0.5 cutoff (the
lenient criterion, $E[\theta_{\text{ROPE}}]>0.5$: equivalence is more probable
than the two alternatives combined --- henceforth $E[\theta]$
denotes the expected posterior mass of the region named in the
sentence, the ROPE in the equivalence readings and the positive
region in the distillation chapter); and the fraction of posterior
samples in which $\theta_{\text{ROPE}}$ is the largest of the three
components, the convention of \citet{benavoli2017}, read with a 0.95
cutoff (the strict criterion). Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} reports
both. Because the prior pseudo-observation sits inside the ROPE, it
pulls small-$N$ results toward equivalence; the radius and its
sensitivity analysis are protocol decisions
(Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}).

\hdrshort{Algorithm selection, regret and dominance}
\section{Algorithm selection, regret and dominance}
\label{sec:fund-selecao}

The \emph{algorithm selection problem} \citep{rice1976} asks whether
the best algorithm for a dataset can be predicted from its
\emph{meta-features} --- properties measurable before training, such
as numbers of rows and attributes or the fraction of categorical
attributes. A \emph{learned router} is a model trained for that task
(each dataset a row, the winner the label); a \emph{fixed policy}
always recommends the same family. Two notions make the comparison
honest. \emph{Leave-one-dataset-out} (LODO) validation trains the
router on $N-1$ datasets and evaluates it on the one left out,
repeating for each --- cross-validation whose held-out unit is the
whole dataset, consistent with the unit of analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:fund-estatistica}. \emph{Regret} measures what is
lost by following a recommendation instead of taking the dataset's
best model; with $m^{*}$ the primary metric of the best model on the
dataset, $m^{-}$ that of the worst, and $m_{F}$ that of the best
model of the recommended family $F$, the \emph{normalized regret} is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{regret}(F) \;=\;
\frac{\lvert m^{*} - m_{F} \rvert}{\lvert m^{*} - m^{-} \rvert},
\label{eq:fund-regret}
\end{equation}
0 when the family contains the best model and 1 when the best it
offers is the worst, so datasets with metrics on different scales
can be aggregated. An \emph{oracle}, which always picks the winner
after seeing the outcome, is not executable and serves only as a
ceiling. With more than one criterion --- accuracy and latency, say
--- a model \emph{dominates} another (in the Pareto sense) if it is
at least as good on every criterion and strictly better on one; the
\emph{Pareto frontier} is the set of non-dominated models, the form
in which Chapters~\ref{chap:benchmark} and~\ref{chap:destilacao}
present their multi-criteria results.

\section{Related work}
\label{chap:related}

The literature this section covers moves in cycles of months, and a
good share of the central works has not undergone peer review; the
characterizations below come from dated online checks made between
July and November 2026 (record in the extended technical report),
and every ``no work does X'' should be read as ``open as far as we
checked, on those dates''.

\subsection{Tabular benchmarks and the GBDT vs.\ deep learning controversy}
\label{sec:rel-benchmarks}

\citet{grinsztajn2022} gave quantitative form to ``trees win on
tables'', attributing the advantage to the irregularity of tabular
target functions and to uninformative attributes;
\citet{mcelfresh2023}, on a larger grid, found that networks win with
non-trivial frequency and that meta-features partially predict when;
\citet{gorishniy2021} had standardized the deep tabular protocol
before both. TabArena \citep{tabarena2025} maintains a living
benchmark; TALENT \citep{talent2024,ye2024closer} standardizes deep
tabular evaluation at scale; TabReD \citep{tabred2024} shows that
conclusions change when the evaluation changes; and a modernized
nearest-neighbor baseline is competitive again \citep{modernnca2025}. On \emph{how} to
compare, the frequentist protocol of \citet{demsar2006} is the
standard; the Bayesian ROPE layer of \citet{benavoli2017} rarely
appears in tabular benchmarks, and the sensitivity of conclusions to
the ROPE radius is almost never analyzed. The two narratives ---
``trees win'' and ``modern deep learning has caught up''
\citep{realmlp2024,tabm2025} --- are, in our reading, supported by
the learning curves of Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}, claims
conditional on sample size and task structure; and the arrival of foundation
models displaces the controversy to pre-trained vs.\ trained from
scratch. The two reference benchmarks predate the second generation
of tabular foundation models, a matter of timing rather than a
defect.

The gap on this axis is not breadth (our benchmark covers fewer
datasets than TabArena) but three fronts: protocol depth per cell
(selection separated from measurement, uniform tuning, frequentist
\emph{and} Bayesian inference with a ROPE sensitivity analysis);
multi-criteria evaluation with cost measured under the same protocol;
and the simultaneous inclusion of the 2026 foundation models (TabFM,
measured weeks after release) and of the Kolmogorov--Arnold family.
The study compares individual models, not AutoML ensembling systems
such as AutoGluon \citep{erickson2020}. TabPFN-3.5, released later,
is outside the roster by dating; TabPFN-3 and TabICLv2
\citep{tabpfn3-2026,tabiclv2-2026}, which predate the roster's
closing, were not considered in that expansion --- a limitation of
the roster, not an effect of dating.

\subsection{Tabular foundation models and their evaluation}
\label{sec:rel-tfm}

TabPFN v2 \citep{hollmann2025} and v2.5 \citep{priorlabs2025}
established ICL with prior-fitted networks for tables; the line
evolved quickly, to TabPFN-3 \citep{tabpfn3-2026} and, more
recently, TabPFN-3.5 \citep{tabpfn35tr2026}, and inspired variants
for scale and speed: TabICL \citep{tabicl2025}, TabFlex
\citep{tabflex2025} with linear attention, and MotherNet
\citep{mothernet2023}, which amortizes fitting into a hypernetwork.
TabFM \citep{tabfm2026}, by Google Research, released with weights in
June 2026, is an independent implementation of the same paradigm;
since late September 2026 there is an official technical report
\citep{kong2026}, still without peer review, which confirms the
scalar regression head. We frame it as an \emph{inter-laboratory
replication} of the PFN/ICL paradigm, not a second paradigm.

TabFM's public numbers at release came from the proponent itself,
with only partial independent verification; \citet{ye2025tabpfnv2}
dissect TabPFN~v2 in depth, a single-model study. A recent line
evaluates tabular foundation models as conditional density
estimators with proper scoring rules \citep{izbicki2026,landsgesell2026},
and ScoringBench \citep{scoringbench2026} does so on 101 regression
datasets, finding that point and probabilistic rankings diverge ---
independent corroboration of the distinction that structures
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}. These are distributional evaluations
of the \emph{teacher}, without any distillation or serving
component; an astronomical application \citep{photoz2026} explicitly
notes that full-catalog deployment may require distillation. As far
as we checked, there was no neutral TabPFN~$\times$~TabFM comparison
under an identical protocol (same partitions, preprocessing, the
same 50{,}000-row context cap and the same structural exclusion of
the 100-class dataset); Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} provides it.

\subsection{Kolmogorov--Arnold networks on tabular data}
\label{sec:rel-kan}

KAN's original evidence \citep{kan2024} concentrates on symbolic
regression and scientific problems. TabKAN \citep{tabkan2025}
claims its best configuration (ChebyKAN) beats XGBoost and tabular
transformers on ten datasets --- average AUC of 0.857 against
XGBoost's 0.814 on the eight binary ones --- but seven of the ten have
fewer than 8{,}000 samples, there is no regression, and the XGBoost
baseline appears undertuned (AUC 0.726 on \texttt{credit\_g}, where
our tuning obtains 0.767 on the hold-out); TabKANet
\citep{tabkanet2024} uses KAN layers only as a numeric embedding.
The critical literature points the other way: \citet{kancritical2024}
find the MLP winning on six of eight machine-learning tasks, and
\citet{poeta2024}, the first independent tabular KAN-vs-MLP
benchmark, has no GBDT baselines and trains for ten epochs. The open
gap is a test of the family against the full GBDT/DL/foundation-model
slate under a uniform multi-criteria protocol, which
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} provides --- with the caveat that
TabKAN is our own reimplementation (Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia});
\citet{poeta2024} is earlier in the isolated comparison, and our
claim is scoped around it.

\subsection{Acceleration and serving of tabular foundation models}
\label{sec:rel-serving}

Inference cost is the family's main operational weakness, and a
distillation-free acceleration literature grew fast in 2026. TACO
\citep{taco2026} learns to compress the context to about 1\% of its
size, with inference up to 94$\times$ faster; CRUMB
\citep{crumb2026} selects a compact context by distributional
matching (MMD) without retraining. Between September and October
2026 came the replacement of up to 96\% of the transformer's blocks
by a closed-form affine translator \citep{shallow2026}, activation
alignment for reduced contexts \citep{actalign2026}, and the
\emph{Fast} variant of TabPFN-3.5 \citep{tabpfn35tr2026} ---
evaluated, as far as we checked, only on classification or point
prediction, except that TabPFN-3.5-Fast is also scored
distributionally by its authors (CRPS), as a faster teacher --- not
a distilled student, and not on a crossed serving frontier against
other strategies. MotherNet and MotherTree \citep{mothernet2023,mothertree2026}
emit fast predictors with neither teacher nor distillation; FP8
attention quantization \citep{kubler2026} is a lever we do not
measure; and conformal calibration of the teacher itself
\citep{park2026} buys coverage guarantees at the teacher's latency.
Truncating the context and shrinking the inference ensemble are
current practice but rarely measured against these proposals or
against each other. The gap is one of cross-comparison: each work
evaluates its strategy against the full teacher, and none measures
distill vs.\ compress the context vs.\ shrink the ensemble on the
same hold-out sets under the same cost axes ---
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} does.

\subsection{Distillation of tabular foundation models}
\label{sec:rel-destilacao}

The work that establishes the distillation of tabular foundation
models is Pocket FM \citep{pocketfm2026}: XGBoost, CatBoost,
LightGBM and MLP students trained on soft labels from multiple
teachers (TabICLv2, TabPFNv2.6, LimiX, Orion-MSP) over 153 datasets,
retaining 96.5\% of the teacher's AUC (a specific configuration, not
an average) with speedups of 38--860$\times$. It adopts and argues
for stratified OOF labeling and analyzes when distillation
works (gains concentrated at low dimensionality); its clinical sister
paper \citep{pocketfmhealth2026} adds calibration and fairness on 19
health datasets. Both are \textbf{classification only}, as are GEAR
\citep{gear2026} and TabDistill \citep{tabdistill2025}; the
distillation of TabPFN into a compact feed-forward network for a
loan-decision simulation carries a classification head and a
point-regression head \citep{dey2026}; distillation into GAMs targets
interpretability, not speed \citep{tfmgam2026}; and Prior Labs sells a
closed distillation engine \citep{priorlabs2025} with \textbf{no
public methodology or code}. A public repository distills TabFM into
XGBoost, but only for classification and point
regression.\footnote{TabFMKD,
\url{https://github.com/gegen07/TabFMKD} (last commit 2026-09-14;
accessed 2026-11-23).}
Contemporary with this work, and postdating the conclusion of our
experiments, \citet{jeong2026} extend distillation on the
regression side to \textbf{point targets} only: squared-error
transfer of the teacher's means, evaluated on RMSE, with stronger
teachers (TabPFN~v3 and TabICLv2) and a different protocol,
reporting point gains; they touch neither quantiles, nor CRPS, nor
calibration, nor a strategy frontier. Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}
discusses how those gains relate to its scoped negative result on
point distillation.

Outside the tabular world, distilling distributions is an
established line --- the Bayesian posterior predictive
\citep{korattikara2015}, ensemble distributions in classification
\citep{malinin2020end2} and regression \citep{malinin2020rpn},
uncertainty in vision \citep{shen2021}. Within the tabular world,
uncertainty has been distilled from a deep ensemble in regression,
evaluated by CRPS and coverage \citep{park2025uncertainty}. In both
lines the teachers are of accessible weights or members, the targets
parametric or sample-based, and the students neural; pre-TFM distillation into boosted trees covers
regression only at the point level \citep{fakoor2020}. The pieces
exist separately: the pinball loss \citep{koenker1978}, CRPS and its
grid estimator \citep{gneiting2007,gneiting2011}, native
multi-quantile objectives in XGBoost (since version 2.0) and arbitrary
quantiles exposed by TabPFN v2.5. The gap is their composition:
\textbf{distributional distillation of a tabular foundation model
into a tree-ensemble student, measured against a pinball-trained
control and evaluated by CRPS and coverage}. A narrower point: TabFM
already appears as a teacher in the public repository above,
restricted to classification and point regression;
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} tests it as a \emph{distributional}
teacher, and its scalar regression head \citep{kong2026} makes the
answer architecturally negative, with its ensemble spread checked
only on the binarized-target
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$ (the dagger
marks the binarized target throughout;
Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}). What we do not claim:
the formulation ``distill a tabular foundation model into a fast
student'' belongs to Pocket FM, and this is not the first
distillation of tabular foundation models.

\subsection{Algorithm selection and meta-learning}
\label{sec:rel-selecao}

Algorithm selection from meta-features goes back to
\citet{rice1976}; \citet{mcelfresh2023} is its modern version for
trees vs.\ networks. Historically, learned routers overfit outside
large dataset collections, and independent evidence from the
foundation-model era points the same way: global meta-feature
approaches do not prove robust for selecting among models of that
generation \citep{herre2026}. What practice lacks is honest
validation of the recommender in the regime real benchmarks occupy
--- with $N{=}18$ datasets, explicit LODO validation, which the
recommendation literature rarely reports in that regime.
Chapter~\ref{chap:framework} runs it and, accordingly, orients the
framework to verifiable constraints rather than learned routing.

\section{Synthesis of the gaps}
\label{sec:rel-sintese}

Table~\ref{tab:lacunas} consolidates the axes: what the literature
offers, the gap found in the dated checks, and where this
dissertation attacks it. None is a gap of formulation --- the
mechanics of distillation is classical and the benchmark protocols
are consolidated; the gaps are of measurement under a uniform
protocol. Two were partially eroded during the project, and both are
scoped accordingly: \citet{poeta2024} anticipated an independent KAN
test in a narrow scope, and \citet{jeong2026} reached point
regression.


The three research questions map onto these rows. RQ1 attacks the
first three: a neutral, multi-criteria comparison that includes the
2026 foundation models and the Kolmogorov--Arnold family under one
protocol (Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}). RQ2 attacks the
algorithm-selection row: a learned router tested under LODO with
small $N$, and fixed and constraint-driven policies evaluated by
regret (Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}). RQ3 attacks the two remaining
rows: the cost inversion that the benchmark quantifies is the
problem that distributional distillation, and the crossed serving
frontier, address (Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}). The gaps are
distinct but chained --- the benchmark produces the evidence the
framework organizes, and the cost it measures is what the
distillation attacks.
