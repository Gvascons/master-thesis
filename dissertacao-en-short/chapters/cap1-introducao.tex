\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

\section{Context and motivation}
\label{sec:intro-contexto}

``On tabular data, gradient boosting always wins.'' Few maxims of
machine-learning practice have been repeated as often over the last
decade, and this one even acquired a quantitative form: a systematic
study identified the inductive biases that explain why tree ensembles
beat neural networks on typical tables \citep{grinsztajn2022}, and
larger-scale evaluations nuanced the verdict without overturning it
\citep{mcelfresh2023}. For the practitioner, the maxim became a
decision shortcut: faced with a table, use XGBoost and move on.

Consider, however, the decision the maxim is meant to settle. A
data-science team must choose the model that will decide credit
approvals in production (a problem in the family of the
\texttt{credit\_g} and
\texttt{give\_\allowbreak me\_\allowbreak some\_\allowbreak credit}
datasets of this study), or the one that will estimate prices and
durations together with prediction intervals, as in the real-estate
datasets \texttt{miami\_housing} and \texttt{kings\_county} of the
distillation study. In 2026 that team faces a menu
the maxim never anticipated: besides the consolidated
gradient-boosting implementations, there are deep architectures
claiming to have caught up, Kolmogorov--Arnold networks with strong
claims of superiority and, at the center of attention, tabular
foundation models --- pre-trained networks that dispense with
task-specific training, because the training set enters as context
at prediction time \citep{hollmann2025,tabfm2026}. Each proponent
publishes numbers in its own favor, measured under differing
protocols and tuning budgets and, increasingly, without peer review.
What the team lacks is evidence produced under a single protocol and
a procedure to turn it into a choice.

This dissertation produces both, and the picture that emerges
contradicts the maxim on two counts. Among the models that preceded
the 2026 generation, the top of the accuracy ranking in binary
classification is a statistical tie whose leader is already a
foundation model; the 2026 generation then shifts the top in an
unexpected direction --- what moved ahead was not tuned deep learning
but pre-training --- through a lead that is consistent across
datasets but not confirmed as a statistical separation. The price
changes the nature of the decision: the accuracy leader is about five
orders of magnitude slower to serve than XGBoost on the reference
dataset. The practical
question becomes a different one: does the leader's latency fit the
use case and, if it does not, what then?

That question gives the dissertation its third line of work. The
obstacle is architectural (the training set travels with the model
on every prediction), and architectural costs can, in principle, be
transferred away: knowledge distillation \citep{hinton2015} trains,
on an expensive teacher's outputs, a student that serves fast. For
tabular foundation models this route was established for
classification \citep{pocketfm2026}; what it transfers in
\emph{regression}, when the target is the full predictive
distribution with quantiles and calibrated intervals, had no academic
answer as far as our dated checks reached.

\section{Research problem}
\label{sec:intro-problema}

The problem can be stated in three layers, each building on the
previous one. The first is a \emph{lack of comparable evidence}: the
families of tabular models available in 2026 --- gradient boosting;
deep learning in its three lineages (dense networks, attention
architectures, Kolmogorov--Arnold networks); and in-context-learning
foundation models --- had not been measured under a single protocol
combining hyperparameter selection separated from measurement,
statistical inference able to \emph{establish} equivalences rather
than only reject them, and cost axes (training time, tuning cost,
serving latency) measured under the same conditions as accuracy. The
reference benchmarks predate the 2026 generation
\citep{grinsztajn2022,mcelfresh2023}, and that generation's numbers
came mostly from its own proponents.

The second layer is \emph{decision}: benchmarks describe, but teams
must choose. The algorithm-selection literature suggests learning a
router from meta-features of the problem \citep{rice1976}; before
recommending one, it must be tested out of sample in the realistic
regime of a few dozen datasets, a test the literature rarely reports.
Until it meets data that played no part in its construction, a
decision artifact remains a hypothesis.

The third layer is the single operational obstacle to which evidence
and decision point together: the latency of the new accuracy leader.
The problem becomes one of determining what knowledge distillation
can and cannot transfer from a tabular foundation model in
regression, at what serving cost, and under which conditions
verifiable \emph{a priori} --- with distributional regression as the
central focus, the axis along which the teacher carries information
that the raw labels do not hand to the student.

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:intro-qps}

The general objective is to establish, under a single protocol with
selection separated from measurement, comparative evidence on the
three families of tabular models in the era of foundation models, and
to convert it into evaluated decision instruments --- including how
far distillation can remove the leading family's main operational
obstacle. Three research questions organize the work:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] \emph{Does the accuracy tie among tabular model
    families survive a rigorous protocol (hold-out untouched by
    selection, Bayesian practical equivalence, cost axes) and the
    arrival of the 2026 generation of foundation models?}
  \item[RQ2.] \emph{Which decision artifact can a benchmark with a
    realistic $N$ of datasets actually support, a learned router or a
    constraint-based policy --- and how does that answer change when
    the decision weighs accuracy against cost?}
  \item[RQ3.] \emph{What does knowledge distillation transfer from a
    tabular foundation model in regression, at what serving cost, and
    under which conditions verifiable a priori?}
\end{description}

The ``tie'' of RQ1 is the state of the literature immediately
preceding this study: on a good share of tabular datasets the
difference between gradient boosting and tuned deep learning is small
and the winner alternates with the dataset \citep{mcelfresh2023}. The
``realistic'' $N$ of RQ2 is the regime of a few dozen datasets in
which any operational benchmark lives. The answers form a chain
(Table~\ref{tab:intro-qps}): the tie and its shift (RQ1) make the
multi-criteria decision unavoidable (RQ2), and the decision policy
runs into a single obstacle, latency, whose reduction is the object
of RQ3. The falsifiable hypotheses are stated where they are tested;
E1--E3 denote the three registered complementary experiments
(E1, classical distributional baselines; E2, weighted multi-criteria
regret; E3, decision utility).

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro-contribuicoes}

The contributions lie in measurement under a uniform protocol and in
openly reported evaluation, out of sample wherever something is
learned; no new learning mechanism is claimed.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A multi-criteria benchmark of the foundation-model
    era}: 14 models $\times$ 18 datasets (250 of the 252 cells; two
    structural exclusions), with a hold-out untouched by selection,
    frequentist and Bayesian inference and three cost axes. In the
    11-model core --- the eleven models that entered the study before
    the 2026 extension --- the top of binary classification is a
    statistical tie: under a ROPE of $\pm$0.01 AUC, 8 of the 10
    non-leaders are practically equivalent to the leader under the
    lenient criterion, 3 of 10 under the strict 0.95 cutoff (the
    reading holds for ROPE $\geq 0.01$) --- and the leader is
    already a foundation model, TabPFN
    (mean rank 3.2). With the 2026 generation, TabFM has the best
    mean rank in all three tasks (in multiclass, over the two
    datasets where it operates) and is the best model on 13 of 18
    datasets (operating on 17): a consistent lead that the
    underpowered tests do not confirm as a separation, at about five
    orders of magnitude more latency on the reference dataset
    (43{,}262 against 0.33~\us/row). It includes, as far as our dated
    checks reach, the first neutral TabPFN~$\times$~TabFM comparison
    under an identical protocol (Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}).
  \item \textbf{An independent evaluation of Kolmogorov--Arnold
    networks}: in the first test of the family against the full
    GBDT/deep-learning/foundation-model grid in a multi-criteria
    benchmark (as far as our dated checks reach), under a uniform
    protocol with tuning under paired conditions, KAN and TabKAN finish in the
    bottom third on all three tasks, though cheap to serve; the
    original claims do not reproduce in our reimplementations
    (TabKAN reimplemented, KAN adapted) under this protocol
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}).
  \item \textbf{A decision framework that is evaluated, not merely
    described}: the learned component is evaluated under
    leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) and fails --- meta-feature routing
    scores 0.11 against 0.44 for the trivial majority rule (11-model
    study; 0.72 against 0.78 with 14); the fixed
    policies are compared descriptively by regret, with ``foundation
    model first'' (an intra-family oracle) at zero median regret in
    the 2026 generation, a median the executable always-TabFM policy
    also attains; experiment E2 locates the inversion of that
    recommendation at 10--20\% of the weight on latency, by median
    regret under rank normalization (20--35\% under the alternative
    normalizations); the
    flowchart is the synthesis of these studies, not an evaluated
    policy (Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}).
  \item \textbf{Distributional distillation of a tabular foundation
    model}: with TabPFN-2.5 as teacher, in regression, \emph{point}
    distillation does not transfer (H1, the point student retaining
    at least half the teacher's advantage: refuted), while distilling the
    quantile curves retains 12--19\% of the teacher's CRPS advantage
    at the median (median CRPS gain 2.8\%, shrinking to 1.4\% with
    both arms re-tuned), positive on 16 of 20 datasets
    ($p\approx0.019$ under the sequential correction; H2 is not
    confirmed as a conjunction, with coverage within $\pm5$~p.p.\ on
    10 of 20), in a student serving at ${\sim}10^2$~\us/row. The
    distilled student also beats the best of QRF and NGBoost (both in
    default configuration) on 17 of 20 datasets (E1; 12 of 20,
    $p=0.25$, against the best teacher-free alternative chosen per
    dataset), and out-of-fold labeling
    of the teacher protects the student's calibration, not its
    accuracy. The results condense into a decision rule whose
    precondition --- an \emph{advantage gate}: the teacher's measured
    advantage over the control must exceed the median across the 20
    datasets --- is computed before the student is trained; the gate
    was verified only in its oracle version, and the rule was derived
    on the same 20 datasets
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}).
  \item \textbf{Reproducible infrastructure and conduct}: pinned
    versions, seeds and data, versioned artifacts to which every
    number traces, registered hypotheses and public error
    correction (Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Publications}
\label{sec:intro-producao}

The research produced two technical reports of the graduate program,
both in English, graded coursework not externally peer reviewed:
\emph{Does ``GBDT Always Win''? A Multi-Criteria Benchmark of 14
Tabular Models --- From a Statistical Tie to the 2026 Shift --- and
an Evaluated Decision Framework} \citep{companion2026}, condensing
Chapters~\ref{chap:benchmark} and~\ref{chap:framework}; and
\emph{Distilling Tabular Foundation Models Beyond Classification:
Distributional Regression and the Distill/Compress/Shrink Pareto
Frontier} \citep{companion-ai2-2026}, condensing
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}. Experiment E2, the per-dataset results
of E1 and the instance-level complementarity analysis are new
relative to both.

\section{Structure of the dissertation}
\label{sec:intro-estrutura}

Chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentacao} gives the technical vocabulary
(model families, in-context learning, distillation, distributional
regression, statistical comparison, algorithm selection and regret)
and positions the work in the literature, closing with the gaps it
attacks. Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia} specifies the protocol shared
by the empirical part, with the design's limitations declared before
any result. Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} answers RQ1: the tie of the
11-model core, the cost axes, the conditional reversals, the shift of
the top by the 2026 generation and the instance-level
complementarity. Chapter~\ref{chap:framework} answers RQ2 with three
evaluation studies and the resulting flowchart.
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} answers RQ3 under a registered
two-stage design. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusao} confronts each
question with the evidence and consolidates contributions,
limitations and future work. Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares}
gathers the per-dataset tables and a map to the extended technical
report --- the long version of this dissertation (181 pages), the
destination of every ``extended report'' remission in this text ---
which, with the code, artifacts and registration documents in the
project repository,\footnote{\url{https://github.com/Gvascons/master-thesis}.}
holds the full process record, ablations and secondary analyses.
