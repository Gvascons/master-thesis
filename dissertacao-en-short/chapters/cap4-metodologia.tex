\chapter{Experimental Methodology}
\label{chap:metodologia}

This chapter fixes the protocol behind the whole empirical part: 14
models from three families evaluated on 18 public OpenML datasets,
with hyperparameters selected by internal cross-validation and every
reported number measured on a hold-out split off before any
selection. It closes, before any result, with the limitations of the
design itself. The distillation design, registered in its own
document, is presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}; here we
describe only the shared data, partitions and hyperparameters it
inherits. Configuration files, per-run artifacts and the full
infrastructure record are in the extended technical report and the
project repository.

\section{Design principles}
\label{sec:met-visao}

A single protocol serves the three research questions: the same
partitions, seeds and per-family budgets feed the benchmark (RQ1),
the decision framework (RQ2) and the distillation (RQ3). Four
principles run through it: strict separation between selection and
measurement (the prior hold-out is never observed by any selection
stage); parity of conditions across models, with the unavoidable
asymmetries --- the foundation models' zero-shot regime, the unequal
tuning budgets --- declared and measured rather than hidden; the
dataset, never the fold, as the unit of statistical analysis; and
traceable provenance of every number to a versioned artifact. What
is inferential and what is descriptive is fixed here: the tests are
read on the ten binary-classification datasets, while multiclass
($N=3$) and regression ($N=5$) are read descriptively; the
pre-registered hypotheses with explicit decision criteria are those
of the distillation (Table~\ref{tab:destil-hipoteses}) and of the
instance-level complementarity analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:cap5-ato5}).

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:met-datasets}

The benchmark uses \textbf{18 public OpenML datasets}
\citep{vanschoren2014}: 10 binary classification, 3 multiclass
classification and 5 regression (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). They
were chosen to cover the three task types (regression included, with
\texttt{kin8nm} and others drawn from curated suites such as
OpenML-CTR23, \citealp{fischer2023}), a wide range of sample sizes
(from about a thousand samples in \texttt{credit\_g} to 581{,}000 in
\texttt{covertype}), because RQ1 asks, among other things, where the
lead changes hands along the size axis; purely numeric, mixed and one predominantly
categorical high-cardinality case (\texttt{amazon\_\allowbreak
employee}), since the treatment of categoricals historically
separates the families \citep{grinsztajn2022, mcelfresh2023}, and
realistic imbalance, from 1:1 to about 16:1 in classification.
Datasets with more than 100{,}000 samples (\texttt{covertype},
\texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction},
\texttt{give\_\allowbreak me\_\allowbreak some\_\allowbreak credit})
are subsampled to 100{,}000 \emph{before any partitioning}
(stratified in classification, simple random sampling in
regression). Subsampling caps for computational feasibility are common in
tabular benchmarks \citep{grinsztajn2022}; ours is 100{,}000, and it
delimits the regime studied to small-to-medium tabular data.

\section{Models evaluated}
\label{sec:met-modelos}

The benchmark compares \textbf{14 models in three families}
(Table~\ref{tab:modelos}): gradient boosting, the consolidated state
of practice; tabular deep learning, the family with the most recent
proposals and the greatest internal heterogeneity; and foundation
models, the paradigmatic bet of the 2025--2026 generation. Five
models use our own implementations: MLP and FT-Transformer, and
SAINT, STab and TabKAN, reimplemented from the original papers for
lack of a mature package; KAN uses the \emph{efficient-kan} backbone
with documented adaptations (layer normalization before each KAN
layer and a grid range of $[-2, 2]$), because standardized inputs fall
mostly outside the original $[-1, 1]$ grid of \citet{kan2024}. A
weak result from a reimplemented model therefore confounds method
and implementation; sanity checks mitigate the risk --- the TabKAN
reimplementation reaches ROC-AUC 0.914--0.915 on \texttt{adult},
compatible with the approximately 0.90 reported by its authors
\citep{tabkan2025} --- and every protocol deviation is declared
(TabKAN, for instance, is trained with AdamW and mini-batches
instead of full-batch L-BFGS, for uniformity with the other
networks).

The two foundation models enter \textbf{zero-shot}: ``training'' is
conditioning the pre-trained model on the training set by in-context
learning (Section~\ref{sec:fund-fm}). The asymmetry is deliberate ---
the zero-shot regime is the claim under test --- and their inference
parameters are fixed policies, not quantities tuned to our data: an
inference ensemble of 8 members in total (TabPFN's default, mirrored
in TabFM for comparability and cost, although TabFM's library
default is 32 \citep{tabfm2026}), each member conditioned on a context capped at
50{,}000 rows --- a cap enforced in TabPFN by an external
subsampling of the pool (4 distinct subsamples) and in TabFM by the
library itself. TabPFN is used at version 2.5; TabFM at release v1.0.1,
pinned by commit, with the \texttt{tabfm-1.0.0-pytorch} weights,
running on the PyTorch backend in bf16 \citep{tabfm2026}. The
\texttt{helena} dataset (100 classes) exceeds both models' class
limit: the two cells TabPFN$\times$\texttt{helena} and
TabFM$\times$\texttt{helena} are \emph{structural exclusions} ---
native architectural limits, not execution failures --- and the
final matrix has 250 of the 252 possible cells.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:met-protocolo}

Figure~\ref{fig:protocolo} summarizes the protocol; the shaded boxes
mark the data and the stage that no selection stage observes.

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics{images/tikz-cap4-1-en.pdf}
\caption[Experimental protocol]{Experimental protocol: prior hold-out
of 20\%; hyperparameter selection by internal cross-validation (3
folds) on the pool (the remaining 80\% development split, which
supplies folds and validation), executed once, followed
by re-evaluation on 5 external folds with the hyperparameters
fixed.}
\label{fig:protocolo}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Untouched hold-out.} 20\% of each dataset is split off
\emph{before} any cross-validation (stratified in classification;
seed 42); the remaining 80\%, the development partition or
\emph{pool}, feeds all selection. Every final result reported comes
exclusively from the hold-out, so even a subtle leakage error in the
selection loop cannot contaminate it. The 80:20 split balances two finite resources. On the test side,
20\% buys metric precision (Section~\ref{sec:met-limitacoes}, item
3). On the training side, the measured learning curves
(Figure~\ref{fig:cap5-curvas}) show the primary metric already nearly
flat near the cut --- from the grid point closest to half the pool
to the full pool, the median relative improvement is 1.2\% (maximum
5.1\%, RealMLP on \texttt{jannis}) --- yet still rising for the
sample-hungry networks, so ceding more training data to the test
would selectively penalize one family. The split was fixed before
any result and is identical for the 14 models, which preserves the
pairing of the rank comparisons.

\textbf{Tuning once, then re-evaluation --- not nested
cross-validation.} Hyperparameter selection uses \emph{internal}
cross-validation (3 folds) on the full pool, executed \textbf{once}
per (model, dataset) pair; the 5 external folds then re-evaluate
the model with the hyperparameters already fixed, and the networks'
early stopping uses the fold under evaluation itself. This design is
\textbf{not} classical nested cross-validation, in which the search
would repeat inside each external fold. The consequence is declared
precisely: the external-fold metrics are optimistic as
generalization estimates, because selection observed those same
data; they play only internal roles (stability across folds), and
no reported result comes from them --- all come from the prior
hold-out. All classification partitions are stratified (hold-out,
external and internal folds, and the 90/10 train/validation split of
the final fit); regression uses shuffled K-Fold.

\textbf{Budgets of 100/25 trials, with the disparity measured.} The
search uses Optuna with the TPE sampler and MedianPruner
\citep{akiba2019}: \textbf{100 trials for gradient boosting and 25
for deep learning}, optimizing each task's primary metric. The
nominal asymmetry was meant to attenuate the cost disparity: the
design premise estimated a deep-learning trial at about 4$\times$ a
GBDT trial and assumed that 25 TPE trials would cover most of the
landscape --- our own premise, not a claim from the literature. The
realized disparity was larger: the median cost per deep-learning
trial was more than an order of magnitude above the GBDTs' (about
57$\times$ by the ratio of medians), and total search time was
1.1--140$\times$ above, depending on the model pair. The residual
bias favors deep learning in search time --- and deep learning
nonetheless does not win in accuracy; the correct reading is ``asymmetric
budgets by design, imperfect parity measured and disclosed'', not
equalization. One exception: on \texttt{adult}, the 7 legacy
deep-learning models were tuned with 100 trials, kept as a
validation anchor, so the legacy architectures had there 4$\times$
the budget of KAN/TabKAN; the dataset is excluded from the
tuning-cost aggregates, and the bias, in favor of models that still
do not beat the GBDTs there, touches no conclusion. The foundation
models are not tuned. Some selected values touch the edge of the
search space (e.g., \texttt{n\_estimators} close to the 2{,}000
cap), a declared limitation of the space design; the search spaces
are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:espacos-busca}.

\textbf{Early stopping and training regime.} The GBDTs use internal
early stopping (50 rounds on a validation set). The networks share
one regime --- at most 200 epochs, early stopping with patience 20
on the validation loss, batch size 256, AdamW, restoration of the
best state --- so that training schedule is not confounded with
architecture; RealMLP alone keeps its package's schedule (256
epochs), whose tuned defaults are part of the model's claim. Each
trial receives a seed derived deterministically from the global seed
42, so any cell of the matrix is reproducible in isolation. Each
experiment runs in an isolated subprocess; out-of-memory errors
trigger automatic batch-size reduction and, at the limit, pruning of
the trial, and the runner is resumable.

\section{Per-family preprocessing}
\label{sec:met-preproc}

Each family receives the input in the format its literature
prescribes, and every preprocessor is fitted exclusively on each
fold's training data. Gradient boosting gets ordinal encoding and
median imputation, with CatBoost receiving the categorical indices
for its native treatment \citep{prokhorenkova2018}; the foundation
models get ordinal encoding, their internal normalization being part
of the models; the deep models get standardized numeric columns and
one-hot categoricals with a \textbf{cap of 50 categories per
column}. The cap avoids dimensional explosion: without it,
\texttt{amazon\_\allowbreak employee} would reach roughly 11{,}000 to
14{,}000 columns in the training splits (15{,}626 on the full
dataset), exceeding GPU memory for the attention models.

\section{Metrics per task type}
\label{sec:met-metricas}

Each task's primary metric is threshold-free and sensitive to
probabilistic quality: \textbf{ROC-AUC} in binary classification
(independent of threshold and prevalence, on a grid with imbalance
up to 16:1), \textbf{log-loss} in multiclass (a proper scoring rule
that scales to $K$ classes), and \textbf{RMSE} in regression, on the
scale in which the suite ships the target. Secondary metrics --- KS,
log-loss, F1 and accuracy in binary; macro- and weighted F1, OvR-AUC
and accuracy in multiclass; MAE and $R^2$ in regression --- are
reported but do not rank models. Accuracy is misleading under
imbalance --- a trivial classifier ``gets'' 94\% on a 16:1 dataset ---
and is never used in conclusions; KS connects the benchmark to
credit and risk practice. Threshold metrics depend on a cutoff that
belongs to the application, so they rank nothing here; they are
opened, at the standard 0.5 threshold, only for XGBoost, TabPFN and
TabFM in the instance-level analysis (Section~\ref{sec:cap5-ato5}). Across datasets, models are compared
by their \textbf{mean rank} on the primary metric, which assumes
neither normality nor commensurability of the metrics.

\section{Statistical-inference protocol}
\label{sec:met-estatistica}

The tests, presented in Section~\ref{sec:fund-estatistica}, follow
the canonical practice for comparing several models on several
datasets \citep{demsar2006, benavoli2017}, applied in this order:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Unit of analysis: the dataset.} The tests operate on
    $N=10$ (binary), $N=3$ (multiclass) and $N=5$ (regression)
    datasets, never on folds, which are not independent
    (pseudo-replication). The binary tie of Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}
    is measured on $N=10$; multiclass and regression are
    descriptive.
  \item \textbf{Friedman} on the per-dataset ranks, with the
    Iman--Davenport correction, less conservative for small $N$;
    \textbf{Nemenyi} post-hoc with critical-difference diagrams,
    $\mathrm{CD} = q_\alpha \sqrt{k(k+1)/6N}$ \citep{demsar2006}.
  \item \textbf{Pairwise Wilcoxon with Holm correction}, for the
    magnitudes. With $N=5$ regression datasets the smallest
    attainable two-sided $p$ is 0.0625, so rejection at
    $\alpha=0.05$ is mathematically impossible, and after Holm all
    p-values saturate: regression conclusions rest on ranks and
    effect sizes, and the pairwise instrument serves the distillation
    chapter's supplementary readings and the conclusion's
    cross-experiment multiplicity check.
  \item \textbf{Effect size} by paired Cohen's $d$ ($d_z$), and
    \textbf{confidence intervals} by percentile bootstrap with
    $B=10{,}000$ resamples and a fixed seed.
  \item \textbf{Bayesian signed-rank with ROPE}
    \citep{benavoli2017}, because non-rejection does not license
    equivalence. The primary ROPE is $\pm0.01$ of AUC --- a declared
    convention, not a constant of the field --- with sensitivity at
    $\{0.005, 0.01, 0.02\}$. Our original implementation returns
    the expected posterior mass $E[\theta]$; the \citet{benavoli2017}
    convention --- the fraction of posterior samples in which each
    region is the most probable --- is what the reference
    implementation returns. Both are implemented and
    Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} reports both criteria.
  \item \textbf{Variance decomposition ($\eta^2$)} of the ranks into
    between-family, within-family and residual shares; the residual
    bundles the model$\times$dataset interaction and noise, and with
    unbalanced families the between-family share is mechanically
    conservative.
  \item At the instance level, McNemar's exact test on discordant
    pairs \citep{mcnemar1947} serves the pre-registered
    complementarity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:cap5-ato5}).
\end{enumerate}

Two comparison grids coexist. The \textbf{11-model core} gathers
the three GBDTs, seven deep-learning models (MLP, FT-Transformer,
SAINT, TabNet, STab, RealMLP, TabM) and one foundation model
(TabPFN); the \textbf{full 14-model grid} adds TabFM, KAN and
TabKAN. The cut is defined by the models' order of entry into the
study, not by publication year: TabPFN, a foundation model, belongs
to the core. The structural exclusions are declared, never imputed
--- imputing the worst rank would punish a known architectural
limit, imputing the mean would fabricate data; analyses that need a
complete matrix use the fully covered models or exclude
\texttt{helena}, and the foundation models' multiclass ranks are
averages over 2 datasets, a fact repeated in every affected table.

\section{Cost axes and complementary experiments}
\label{sec:met-custo}
\label{sec:met-reprodutibilidade}

Three cost axes complement predictive performance.
\textbf{Training} and \textbf{tuning} cost are the wall-clock time
recorded per experiment, decomposed into hyperparameter search and
final fit. \textbf{Inference latency} is measured in microseconds
per row (\us/row) on the full hold-out of \texttt{adult}, the
reference dataset: median of 5 passes after warm-up, with CUDA
synchronization and an identical batch protocol for the 14 models,
each on its natural device (GBDTs on CPU; deep and foundation models
on GPU). The ratios therefore describe the amortized batch-serving
regime on a declared machine, not single-row online latency. A
control measurement on one small and one large dataset
(\texttt{credit\_g} and \texttt{cardiovascular\_\allowbreak
disease}) checks the stability of the rankings across datasets
(Section~\ref{sec:met-limitacoes}). All experiments run on a single
machine --- an NVIDIA RTX 5080 GPU (16~GB of VRAM) and 32~GB of
RAM, with the GBDTs serving on the CPU --- which is declared
because it delimits the regime studied and because cost is only
interpretable with the machine explicit; reproducibility rests on
fixed seeds, a SHA-256 hash of each dataset recorded per experiment
(against silent changes of the data), corrections by rectification
that preserve history, versioned configurations, pinned
dependencies and public raw results.

Two complementary experiments feed RQ1. \textbf{Learning curves}
re-train six representative models on growing slices of the pool
(from 500 samples to the available total; 3 seeds; 5 datasets),
with hyperparameters fixed at the full-dataset values and the same
hold-out, isolating the effect of sample size at fixed capacity.
\textbf{Instance-level complementarity} compares the per-instance
hold-out predictions of the foundation models and XGBoost under a
pre-registered design (Section~\ref{sec:cap5-ato5}). The decision
framework's leave-one-dataset-out protocol is described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}, and the distillation design in
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}.

\section{Limitations of the experimental design}
\label{sec:met-limitacoes}

The known weaknesses of the design are declared before any result.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Not nested.} Tuning runs once on the pool and the
    external folds reuse its data; their metrics are optimistic and
    feed no reported result (Section~\ref{sec:met-protocolo}).
  \item \textbf{Unequal tuning budget.} 100 trials for GBDTs, 25 for
    deep learning (100 for the 7 legacy deep models on
    \texttt{adult}); the realized cost disparity (about 57$\times$
    per trial) is measured and disclosed, not equalized.
  \item \textbf{A single hold-out partition.} On \texttt{adult}
    (9{,}769 test rows) the bootstrap 95\% CI of the hold-out AUC
    has a half-width of $\pm$0.005; on \texttt{credit\_g} (200
    rows), $\pm$0.074 --- which is why no conclusion rests on a
    single dataset and inference aggregates ranks.
  \item \textbf{Small $N$, low power.} With 10/3/5 datasets per
    task, the regression Wilcoxon has a floor of $p \ge 0.0625$ and
    multiclass is descriptive; the mitigation is to claim no single
    winner, to support equivalences by the Bayesian analysis, and to
    name the limit wherever it binds.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{TotalCharges} on \texttt{telco} treated as
    categorical.} The column arrives from OpenML as text and becomes
    a categorical with 6{,}531 levels, so most of the hold-out gets
    the unknown-category code. The treatment is identical for the 14
    models, but re-evaluating all of them with the column numeric
    changes the AUC by $-0.003$ to $+0.006$ for the other models and
    by $+0.028$ for \textbf{TabFM alone}, which jumps from last
    (0.8233) to the best AUC on the dataset among the 14
    re-evaluated (0.8515; second is LightGBM, 0.8505). The dataset is
    the worst case of the ``always-TabFM'' policy
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}), a reading that carries this
    caveat.
  \item \textbf{Regression target not standardized for the
    networks.} A dedicated measurement (5 networks $\times$ 5
    datasets $\times$ 3 seeds, inherited hyperparameters --- a floor
    on the effect) finds RMSE improvement with standardization in 17
    of the 25 cells, typically below 2\%, largest for the MLP
    ($+9.6\%$ on \texttt{kin8nm}, $+5.3\%$ on
    \texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction}) and TabM ($+3.4\%$ on
    \texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction}), with small degradations
    elsewhere (maximum $-4.3\%$): the deep regression baselines are
    slightly underestimated in those cases.
  \item \textbf{TabPFN's default classifier checkpoint saw real
    data.} The default v2.5 classifier checkpoint is Real-TabPFN-2.5,
    fine-tuned on 43 OpenML/Kaggle datasets that include
    \texttt{higgs} and, by a name match not verified row by row,
    probably \texttt{cardiovascular\_disease}
    \citep{priorlabs2025}; the hold-out protects against our
    leakage, not the pre-training's. Repeating the final fit with
    the synthetic-only checkpoint on the twelve classification
    datasets where TabPFN operates (one seed) gives an AUC
    difference of $-0.0051$ to $+0.0002$, median $-0.0002$; on
    \texttt{higgs} the synthetic checkpoint is slightly better
    (0.8113 against 0.8111), with no sign of memorization. The
    regressor checkpoint is purely synthetic, so the distillation is
    out of this risk's reach; TabFM declares exclusively synthetic
    pre-training \citep{kong2026}. A residual, unaddressable risk
    remains: priors and inference policies tuned across versions by
    teams that know the public benchmarks. It is mitigated by stating
    conclusions about foundation models at the regime level
    (consistency across datasets), not per cell, and by keeping the
    cost axis, immune to contamination, in every practical
    conclusion.
  \item \textbf{Pinned, unreviewed TabFM.} Results refer to release
    v1.0.1; its technical report \citep{kong2026} has not undergone
    peer review.
  \item \textbf{Reimplementations.} SAINT, STab and TabKAN are
    reimplemented and KAN adapted (Section~\ref{sec:met-modelos});
    a weak result from them confounds method and implementation.
  \item \textbf{Double exclusion of \texttt{helena}.} Both
    foundation models' multiclass ranks average 2 datasets. It is a
    native limit, not an impossibility --- TabPFN-2.5 offers an
    official \texttt{ManyClassClassifier} --- and the exclusion keeps
    the models in their native zero-shot form; removing
    \texttt{helena} instead would hide the kind of architectural
    restriction a study of practical decision-making should expose.
  \item \textbf{Latency measured on one reference dataset and one
    regime.} The control measurement on \texttt{credit\_g} and
    \texttt{cardiovascular\_\allowbreak disease} finds stable
    latency rankings (Spearman 0.78--0.95) and the expected context
    dependence of the foundation models (TabPFN: 840~\us/row on the
    smallest, 45{,}578 on the largest). The numbers do not transfer
    to single-row serving or other hardware; the orders of
    magnitude between families are expected to transfer, but
    hardware sensitivity was not measured.
  \item \textbf{Learning curves with fixed hyperparameters.}
    Reusing the full-dataset hyperparameters on all slices isolates
    the size effect at fixed capacity, but may disfavor models whose
    optimal configuration varies strongly with $n$.
  \item \textbf{Delimited external validity.} Eighteen datasets, the
    100{,}000-sample cap and a single 16~GB GPU delimit the regime
    to small and medium data; Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} extends
    the regression evaluation to OpenML-CTR23 partly to probe this.
    TabICLv2 and TabPFN-3 \citep{tabiclv2-2026,tabpfn3-2026}, which
    predate the roster's closing, were not considered --- a
    limitation of the roster, not an effect of dating.
  \item \textbf{Bayesian reading criteria fixed after the analysis
    began.} The ROPE radius and the two decision cutoffs were fixed
    after the analysis of the results had begun, not pre-registered;
    both are reported with a sensitivity sweep
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}).
  \item \textbf{Construct validity.} Threshold-free primary metrics
    reduce the risk of optimizing an inadequate proxy, but any scalar
    metric compresses
    what an operator weighs; the decision framework keeps cost and
    performance as separate axes instead of aggregating them
    prematurely.
\end{enumerate}

With data, models, partitions, metrics, tests and limitations fixed,
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} executes the protocol on the matrix's
250 cells.
