\chapter{Theoretical Background}
\label{chap:fundamentacao}

This chapter builds, from the concrete to the abstract, the technical
vocabulary the rest of the dissertation needs: tabular data and their
constitutive heterogeneity, the metrics that measure a classifier and
the trade-offs among them, decision trees and gradient boosting, the
three lineages of tabular deep learning, foundation models and
in-context learning, knowledge distillation, the toolkit of
distributional regression, and the statistical tests for algorithm
comparison together with the decision vocabulary. Depth follows use:
the benchmark's architectures are described by mechanism; in-context
learning, distributional regression, statistical inference and
decision receive the detail needed to read the results without
consulting the sources.

\section{Tabular data and supervised learning}
\label{sec:fund-tabular}

Let us start with the object. A \emph{tabular dataset} is a table in
which each row is an observation and each column an attribute --- the
format of a bank statement, a medical record, or a customer registry.
Table~\ref{tab:adult-exemplo} illustrates the format with rows in the
style of the \texttt{adult} dataset, a classic of the field in which
the goal is to predict, from sociodemographic census attributes,
whether a person's annual income exceeds \$50{,}000
\citep{kohavi1996}.

\begin{table}[htb]
\centering
\caption[Illustrative fragment of a tabular dataset]{Illustrative
fragment, with fictitious rows, of a tabular dataset in the style of
\texttt{adult}. Source: the author.}
\label{tab:adult-exemplo}
\begin{tabular}{rllrl}
\toprule
age & education & occupation & hours/week & income $>$ 50K? \\
\midrule
39 & college       & management  & 45 & yes \\
23 & high school   & services    & 40 & no \\
51 & graduate      & specialist  & 50 & yes \\
34 & high school   & ?           & 38 & no \\
28 & college       & technician  & 44 & no \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\emph{Heterogeneity is constitutive.} Table~\ref{tab:adult-exemplo}
already exhibits everything that makes the format hard: \texttt{age}
and \texttt{hours/week} are numbers on different scales;
\texttt{education} is a category with a natural order;
\texttt{occupation} is a \emph{nominal} category, with neither order
nor distance between values, and of arbitrary cardinality; and the
``?'' is a missing value whose absence may itself carry information.
A single table therefore mixes continuous variables, counts,
categories, and gaps --- and each column is an independent semantic
axis: swapping two columns does not change the problem, but swapping
two pixels of an image does.

\emph{Why did the vision and language revolutions not transfer?}
Convolutional networks exploit the spatial structure of images
(neighboring pixels are correlated; patterns repeat at any position);
language Transformers exploit the sequential structure of text.
Tabular data offer no analogous universal invariance that an
architecture could exploit \emph{by construction} --- there is no
notion of neighborhood between \texttt{age} and \texttt{occupation}.
That absence of shared structure is the usual explanation for a
persistent empirical fact: for more than a decade, the state of the
art on tables belonged to another family of models, the ensembles of
decision trees \citep{grinsztajn2022}. Sections~\ref{sec:fund-gbdt}
and~\ref{sec:fund-dl} develop the two sides of that dispute.

Let us formalize the bare minimum of the supervised-learning
problem. Given a training set
$D = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ of observation--target pairs,
assumed to be independent samples from an unknown distribution, the
goal is to learn a function $f$ that predicts the target of \emph{new}
observations well --- that is, that minimizes the expected error under
the generating distribution, not the error on the training set itself.
The distinction is the heart of the problem: a sufficiently flexible
model can drive the training error to zero simply by memorizing $D$
(\emph{overfitting}), without generalizing. This is why every honest
evaluation measures performance on data that the model --- and,
equally important, the model-\emph{selection} process --- has never
seen; Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia} translates this principle into
protocol (a test set split off before anything else, internal
cross-validation for hyperparameter selection, with the role of each
loop declared).

This work considers the three canonical types of supervised task on
tables: \emph{binary classification} (predicting one of two classes,
as in Table~\ref{tab:adult-exemplo}), \emph{multiclass
classification} (predicting one of $K$ classes) and \emph{regression}
(predicting a continuous value). Regression admits two readings that
this dissertation distinguishes with care: the \emph{point} reading,
in which the model returns a single number (an estimate of the
conditional mean), and the \emph{distributional} reading, in which
the model returns a complete predictive distribution --- quantiles,
intervals, densities --- from which calibration is demanded. The
distinction may seem subtle now, but it is the axis of the
contribution of Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}, and
Section~\ref{sec:fund-distribucional} gives it its own toolkit. It is
worth fixing right away a distinction that avoids a common
misunderstanding: the score a classifier returns (a number in
$[0,1]$, such as the estimated probability that the row belongs to
the positive class) is a statement about a \emph{discrete} target,
and bears no relation to the predictive distribution of a continuous
target --- the latter lives in the unit of the target itself, and it
is what the quantiles of Section~\ref{sec:fund-distribucional} are
about.

\section{Measuring a classifier: from the confusion matrix to ROC-AUC}
\label{sec:fund-metricas-clf}

Before introducing the competitors, let us fix the yardstick. The
whole comparison of Chapters~\ref{chap:benchmark}
and~\ref{chap:framework} depends on measuring classifiers, and the
metrics involved are not interchangeable: each answers a different
question, and choosing the wrong one can crown a useless model. This
section builds the classification metrics from the most elementary
object of evaluation --- the confusion matrix --- and justifies the
choice that anchors the benchmark.

In a binary problem, call \emph{positive} the class of interest ---
in \texttt{adult}, income above \$50{,}000; in a diagnosis,
the disease; in credit, the default. Confronted with the true label,
each prediction falls into one of four cells: \emph{true positive}
(TP, the model flags it and it is), \emph{false positive} (FP, flags
it and it is not), \emph{false negative} (FN, does not flag it and it
is) and \emph{true negative} (TN). The $2\times 2$ table of these
counts is the \emph{confusion matrix}, and every decision metric is a
ratio between its cells \citep{hastie2009,fawcett2006}.

The most immediate ratio is \emph{accuracy},
$(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{TN})/n$ --- the fraction of correct
predictions. It is also the most treacherous, because it ignores
\emph{how} the errors are distributed between the classes. The
example is not hypothetical: on the \texttt{amazon\_employee} dataset
of this benchmark, in which only 5.8\% of access requests are denied,
XGBoost reaches 94.3\% accuracy on the hold-out --- and detects only
3.2\% of the requests that should have been denied. A model that
answered ``approved'' to every request would have nearly identical
accuracy and zero utility. Under imbalance, accuracy mostly measures
prevalence, not the model.

The metrics that see this asymmetry look at the classes separately.
\emph{Recall} (or \emph{sensitivity}),
$\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})$, asks: \emph{of the positive
cases that exist, how many did the model find?} \emph{Precision},
$\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})$, asks the converse: \emph{of
the alarms the model raised, how many were true?}
\emph{Specificity}, $\mathrm{TN}/(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})$, is the
recall of the negative class; \emph{balanced accuracy} is the mean
of sensitivity and specificity, immune to prevalence; and the
harmonic mean of precision and recall, the \emph{F1}, summarizes the
pair in one number --- useful as a synthesis, opaque as a diagnosis,
because it hides \emph{which} of the two components failed.

Which of them matters depends on the cost asymmetry of the problem,
which is a property of the application, not of the model. In cancer
screening, the false negative is the expensive error --- telling a
sick person ``all is well'' delays treatment --- and the guard
metric is recall, even at the price of more false alarms; in a spam
filter, it is the false positive that is intolerable (a legitimate
contract in the trash), and precision rules. This is why reporting a
single threshold metric, without declaring the cost that justifies
it, is telling half the story --- and why the benchmark reports them
jointly (Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/metricas_clf_diss_en.png}
\caption[ROC curve and threshold choice]{ROC curve of XGBoost on the
\texttt{adult} hold-out (a) and precision and recall as functions of
the threshold (b). Source: the author.}
\label{fig:roc-adult}
\end{figure}

There is, however, a fact the confusion matrix hides: it is not a
property of the model, but of the model--threshold pair. The
classifiers in this work return a \emph{probability}
$\hat p(\mathbf{x})$, and the matrix is only born when one chooses
the cutoff that converts probability into decision. Moving the
cutoff slides errors from one type to the other: on \texttt{adult},
lowering it from 0.5 to 0.25 raises recall from 0.648 to 0.860 at
the cost of more than tripling the false-positive rate (from 0.052
to 0.171); raising it to 0.75 makes the opposite move
(Figure~\ref{fig:roc-adult}b). None of these points is ``the''
performance of the model --- they are different operating points of
the same model.

The \emph{ROC curve} resolves the arbitrariness by sweeping all
thresholds at once: for each cutoff, it plots the true-positive rate
(recall) against the false-positive rate ($1-$ specificity); the
resulting trace describes everything the model can offer, from
conservative to liberal (Figure~\ref{fig:roc-adult}a). The area
under that curve, the \emph{ROC-AUC}, summarizes the quality of the
\emph{ranking} in one number with an exact interpretation: it is the
probability that a randomly drawn positive receives a higher score
than a randomly drawn negative \citep{hanley1982}. Chance scores
0.5; perfection, 1. Because it depends on neither threshold nor
prevalence \citep{fawcett2006}, AUC compares models --- not
operating points --- and therefore anchors the benchmark's
conclusions, with the threshold metrics as a complementary reading
(Section~\ref{sec:met-metricas}).

AUC has its own blind spot: it measures the \emph{order} of the
scores, not their value --- a model can rank perfectly by saying
0.51 for every positive and 0.49 for every negative, with
probabilities that are useless as probabilities. The quantity that
penalizes this is the \emph{log-loss},
$-\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \log \hat p_i(y_i)$, a \emph{proper scoring
rule}: its expected value is minimized by reporting the true
probability \citep{gneiting2007}, which punishes both error and
badly calibrated confidence --- and makes it the natural primary
metric of the multiclass case, where ``positive'' is no longer
unique and the rates above are read per class (macro-averages). This
same demand --- getting the uncertainty right, not only the order
--- reappears on a continuous scale in distributional regression,
whose toolkit Section~\ref{sec:fund-distribucional} develops.

\section{From decision trees to gradient boosting}
\label{sec:fund-gbdt}

\subsection{One tree, one guided example}

A \emph{decision tree} predicts through a sequence of questions
about the attributes, each with a binary answer, until reaching a
leaf that holds the prediction \citep{cart1984}.
Figure~\ref{fig:arvore-exemplo} shows a small tree, of depth two,
trained (illustratively) on data like those of
Table~\ref{tab:adult-exemplo}.

\includegraphics{images/tikz-cap2-1-en.pdf}
\caption[Illustrative decision tree]{Illustrative decision tree, of

with income $>$ 50K. Source: the author.}
\label{fig:arvore-exemplo}
\end{figure}

Follow the third row of Table~\ref{tab:adult-exemplo} through the
tree: the person has a graduate degree (education $\geq$ college:
\emph{yes}, right branch) and is 51 years old (age $>$ 30:
\emph{yes}), landing in the leaf with $\hat{p} = 0.61$ --- the tree
estimates a 61\% probability of high income and, with a 50\%
threshold, predicts ``yes''. Training builds these questions from
the top down, choosing at each node, by greedy search, the attribute
and cutoff that best separate the classes (according to an impurity
criterion such as entropy or the Gini index); in regression, the
leaves hold means and the criterion is variance reduction.

Two properties of this construction deserve note right away, because
they will reappear as explanations of empirical results. First, the
partitions are \emph{axis-aligned}: each question involves a single
attribute, which matches the tabular nature in which each column has
its own semantics. Second, the tree is \emph{invariant to monotone
transformations} of each attribute --- asking ``age $>$ 30'' is
equivalent to asking ``$\log(\text{age}) > \log 30$'' --- so that
normalizing columns, a mandatory step for neural networks, is
irrelevant for trees.

\subsection{Why is one tree not enough?}

A single tree faces a dilemma. If it is shallow, it is stable but
coarse: the example of Figure~\ref{fig:arvore-exemplo} reduces the
whole population to four groups. If it is deep, it becomes
expressive but unstable: it memorizes particularities of the
training sample, and a small perturbation of the data produces a
very different tree. In the classical language of the
\emph{bias--variance} decomposition, shallow trees suffer from high
bias and deep trees from high variance \citep{hastie2009}.
\emph{Ensembles} --- committees of trees --- attack each horn of the
dilemma by a different route.

The first route reduces variance in parallel. \emph{Bagging} and its
most celebrated refinement, random forests, train many deep trees on
perturbed replicas of the data (resampling with replacement and
random attribute draws per node) and average the predictions: the
idiosyncratic errors of each tree, approximately independent, cancel
in the average, and the committee's variance plummets without
raising the bias \citep{breiman2001}.

The second route reduces bias in sequence. \emph{Boosting} inverts
the logic: it starts from \emph{shallow} trees (high bias, low
variance) and trains them sequentially, each new tree focused on
correcting the errors the accumulated committee still makes. The
final prediction is the weighted sum of all the corrections. At each
round, the badly predicted examples gain prominence, and the
committee refines exactly the regions where it still errs --- the
bias falls step by step, while the shallowness of each tree and a
small learning step hold the variance down.

Formally, gradient boosting is gradient descent in function space
\citep{friedman2001}: at each round $m$, a tree $h_m$ is fitted to
the negative gradient of the loss function evaluated at the current
predictions (in the case of squared error, to the residuals
$y_i - F_{m-1}(x_i)$), and one updates $F_m = F_{m-1} + \nu\, h_m$
with a small learning rate $\nu$. One consequence matters later:
since only the gradient of the loss enters the algorithm, the same
GBDT can be trained to minimize squared error (and estimate a mean),
the \emph{pinball} loss (and estimate a quantile,
Section~\ref{sec:fund-distribucional}) or several outputs at once
--- which is what allows Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} to use an
XGBoost as a 19-quantile student.

\subsection{XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost: one concrete difference each}

Three modern implementations of gradient boosting over trees
(GBDTs, for gradient-boosted decision trees) dominate practice, and
the benchmark of Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} evaluates all three.
For the purposes of this dissertation, it suffices to retain one
concrete difference of each. \textbf{XGBoost} \citep{chen2016}
consolidated the paradigm with a regularized formulation of the
objective --- explicit penalties on the number of leaves and the
magnitude of the weights --- that disciplines the growth of each
tree. \textbf{LightGBM} \citep{ke2017} changes the growth order:
instead of expanding the tree level by level, it always expands the
\emph{leaf} with the largest gain (leaf-wise growth), with attribute
histograms that make training substantially faster on large data.
\textbf{CatBoost} \citep{prokhorenkova2018} distinguishes itself by
two mechanisms based on random permutations of the examples. The
first is the native treatment of categorical variables through
\emph{ordered target statistics}: the encoding of each category uses
only the ``earlier'' examples in the permutation, avoiding the
target leakage of naive encodings. The second is \emph{ordered
boosting} proper: each example's residuals are estimated by models
trained without it, attacking the prediction-shift bias of classical
boosting.

In practice, a GBDT's behavior is governed by a handful of
hyperparameters with roles interpretable in the light of the
bias--variance dilemma: tree \emph{depth} controls the order of the
interactions each correction can capture; the \emph{number of
trees} and the \emph{learning rate} form a coupled pair (smaller
steps require more steps, but regularize); and the row and column
\emph{subsampling} fractions per tree inject the decorrelation that
bagging taught. No configuration dominates all datasets --- which
is why any fair comparison between models must optimize each one's
hyperparameters under the same budget, as
Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia} specifies.

\emph{Why does boosting dominate tabular data?} Gathering the
threads: axis-aligned partitions capture non-linearities and
per-attribute interactions without assuming any geometry between
columns; invariance to monotone transformations dispenses with
normalization and absorbs heterogeneous scales; greedy cutoff
selection naturally ignores irrelevant attributes; and boosting
composes these virtues into a systematic bias reducer. The result
is consistent performance, with little tuning, exactly in the
regime of heterogeneous data of small to medium size --- a
diagnosis corroborated by the evaluation of
\citet{grinsztajn2022}, with the recorded caveat that
\citet{mcelfresh2023}, on the wider grid, consider the NN~vs.\ GBDT
debate overemphasized: on a surprising number of datasets the
difference is negligible, and light hyperparameter tuning of a GBDT
matters, on average, more than the choice between the two families.
For this reason, in this dissertation the GBDTs play the role of
\emph{null hypothesis}: every new proposal on tabular data must be
measured against them.

\section{Deep learning for tabular data}
\label{sec:fund-dl}

Deep learning's answer to the dominance of GBDTs organized itself
into three lineages: modernizing the MLP, importing the attention of
Transformers and, more recently, rethinking the neuron itself with
Kolmogorov--Arnold networks. This section walks through them in
order.

\subsection{The multi-layer perceptron}

The \emph{multi-layer perceptron} (MLP) is the simplest neural
network: layers of units that linearly combine the inputs and apply
a non-linearity. With input $\mathbf{h}^{(0)} = \mathbf{x}$, each
layer $l$ computes
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{h}^{(l)} \;=\; \phi\!\left(\mathbf{W}^{(l)} \mathbf{h}^{(l-1)}
  + \mathbf{b}^{(l)}\right),
  \label{eq:mlp-camada}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}^{(l)}$ and $\mathbf{b}^{(l)}$ are learned
parameters and $\phi$ is a fixed activation function (typically the
ReLU, $\phi(z) = \max(0, z)$), and the output of the last layer is
read as the prediction,
\begin{equation}
  f(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; \mathbf{W}^{(L)} \mathbf{h}^{(L-1)} + \mathbf{b}^{(L)},
  \label{eq:mlp-saida}
\end{equation}
followed by a \emph{softmax} in classification. The parameters are
adjusted by gradient descent on a loss function, with gradients
computed by backpropagation \citep{rumelhart1986}. In one sentence:
with enough width, an MLP can approximate any continuous function
\citep{hornik1989} --- the question on tabular data was never
expressiveness, but whether training finds, with finite and
heterogeneous data, solutions as good as those of the GBDTs.

Before any layer, there is an awkward decision: how to hand
\texttt{occupation} to a network that only consumes numbers?
\emph{One-hot} encoding --- one binary column per category ---
explodes the dimensionality when cardinality is high: in this
dissertation's benchmark, the \texttt{amazon\_\allowbreak employee}
dataset would reach roughly 11{,}000 to 14{,}000 columns in the
training splits (15{,}626 on the full dataset) without a cardinality
cap, penalizing
especially the attention models, whose cost grows with the number of
columns. The standard alternative is learned \emph{embeddings}: each
category is mapped to a dense low-dimensional vector, trained
together with the network --- a solution all the architectures below
adopt in some form.

\emph{The recipe matters.} Two recent proposals show that much of
the MLP's historical disadvantage was one of \emph{recipe}, not
architecture. \textbf{RealMLP} \citep{realmlp2024} demonstrates that
an MLP with a careful set of improvements --- numeric embeddings,
learning-rate scheduling, tuned regularization and preprocessing ---
competes with tuned GBDTs. \textbf{TabM} \citep{tabm2025} attacks
another flank: it obtains the stabilizing effect of an ensemble of
MLPs at a cost close to that of a single model, by efficiently
parameterizing multiple ``sub-networks'' that share most of their
weights.

If GBDTs dominate, why does the field keep investing in networks?
For two promises trees do not make and one that networks fulfill
more naturally. First, networks learn \emph{representations}:
category embeddings and reusable intermediate layers, the gateway to
transfer between tasks and to mixed data (a table alongside text or
images). Second, and decisive for this dissertation: the whole
family of foundation models of Section~\ref{sec:fund-fm}, which
redefines the cost of entry into new tasks, is only possible on top
of networks. Third, a network swaps its output head with minimal
architectural change --- multi-target, multi-quantile,
distributional. This plasticity is not exclusive to networks:
gradient boosting also optimizes any differentiable loss (that is
its definition), current implementations support native
multi-quantile outputs, and it is a multi-quantile XGBoost that is
the main student of Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} --- but in
networks it comes for free, and the same chapter uses it in the MLP
comparison student.

\subsection{Attention and Transformers}

The second lineage imports the mechanism that revolutionized
language processing: \emph{attention} \citep{vaswani2017}. Here the
mathematical depth is justified: over a third of the benchmark's
models and all the foundation models of Section~\ref{sec:fund-fm}
are built on these equations.

Given a sequence of $n$ input vectors stacked in
$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, attention projects each
vector into three roles --- \emph{query}, \emph{key} and
\emph{value}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_Q, \qquad
  \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_K, \qquad
  \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_V,
  \label{eq:qkv}
\end{equation}
with learned projection matrices, and produces, for each position, an
average of the values weighted by the affinity between its query and
all the keys:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V})
  \;=\; \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(
    \frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}
  \right)\mathbf{V},
  \label{eq:atencao}
\end{equation}
where $d_k$ is the key dimension and the division by $\sqrt{d_k}$
stabilizes the magnitudes before the softmax. The intuition: each
element of the sequence ``asks'' (via $\mathbf{Q}$) which other
elements are relevant to it (via $\mathbf{K}$) and aggregates their
information (via $\mathbf{V}$) in proportion to that relevance ---
weights that are \emph{computed from the data}, not fixed by the
architecture. \emph{Multi-head} attention runs $h$ attentions in
parallel with independent projections and concatenates the results,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{MultiHead}(\mathbf{X}) \;=\;
  \operatorname{concat}(\text{head}_1, \ldots, \text{head}_h)\,
  \mathbf{W}_O,
  \label{eq:multihead}
\end{equation}
allowing different heads to capture different interaction patterns.
A \emph{Transformer block} stacks multi-head attention and a
position-wise MLP, with residual connections and layer
normalization; the original full architecture stacks these blocks
into an \emph{encoder} (which processes the whole input at once,
with unrestricted attention) and a \emph{decoder} (which generates
text token by token, with causal attention).

\emph{Why do tabular models use only the encoder?} Because there is
nothing to \emph{generate} sequentially: the prediction for a row is
a single label or value, not a sequence. Tabular Transformers treat
the \emph{columns} of a row as the input ``sequence'' --- each
attribute becomes a token --- and use only the encoder stack:
attention between attribute tokens learns interactions between
columns, and the prediction is read from a special aggregator token.
The decoder and its causal mask, made for the temporal order of
text, have no counterpart in a table row.

Four attention architectures are part of this work's benchmark.
\textbf{TabNet} \citep{arik2021} pioneered bringing attention to
tables, combining it with \emph{sparse} attribute selection: at each
processing step, a learned mask elects a few relevant attributes,
which gives the model native interpretability. The
\textbf{FT-Transformer} \citep{gorishniy2021} is the cleanest
distillation of the idea: a \emph{Feature Tokenizer} converts each
attribute (numeric or categorical) into an embedding token and a
standard Transformer processes the row; it is the architectural
reference of the lineage. \textbf{SAINT} \citep{somepalli2021} adds
to the attention between columns an attention \emph{between rows}
(\emph{intersample}): when classifying a row, the model can consult
other rows of the batch --- an anticipation, in miniature, of the
idea of using examples as context that Section~\ref{sec:fund-fm}
takes to the extreme. \textbf{STab} \citep{stab-ref} completes the
lineage with a distinct mechanism: instead of deterministic
activations, its layers use \emph{local stochastic competition}
units (LWTA, local winner-takes-all), in which blocks of neurons
compete and a winner is drawn by differentiable Gumbel-softmax
sampling; at inference, the prediction is the Bayesian average of 64
stochastic passes. The cost of this sampling-based inference appears
prominently on the latency axis of Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}.

\subsection{Kolmogorov--Arnold networks}

The third lineage, the most recent, changes where the non-linearity
lives. The Kolmogorov--Arnold representation theorem guarantees that
every continuous function of several variables can be written as a
composition of sums of continuous functions of \emph{one} variable
\citep{kolmogorov1957,arnold1957}. \emph{Kolmogorov--Arnold
Networks} (KANs) \citep{kan2024} take the theorem as architectural
inspiration and invert the MLP's design: instead of fixed
activations at the nodes and scalar weights on the edges
(Eq.~\ref{eq:mlp-camada}), they place \emph{learnable univariate
functions} on the edges --- parameterized by B-splines --- and mere
sums at the nodes. Each connection stops being a number and becomes
a small adjustable curve. Variants swap the basis of the edge
functions (Chebyshev polynomials in ChebyKAN, radial basis functions
in FastKAN), and \textbf{TabKAN} \citep{tabkan2025} organizes these
variants into a framework aimed at tabular data.

The KANs' promise --- more expressiveness per parameter and
interpretability of the learned curves --- met empirical skepticism:
under comparison with parameter and FLOP parity, the MLP beats KANs
on most machine-learning tasks \citep{kancritical2024}; the
independent tabular evaluation of \citet{poeta2024}, in turn,
reached results \emph{favorable} to KAN in accuracy --- but without
GBDT baselines and under ten-epoch training. None of these
evaluations, on either side, includes GBDTs and foundation models in
the same protocol --- the gap Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} fills
under a neutral protocol, with the reimplementation caveats
disclosed in Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}.

\section{Tabular foundation models and in-context learning}
\label{sec:fund-fm}

Everything seen so far shares a premise so basic that it goes
unnoticed: for each new dataset, a new model is trained. Tabular
foundation models abandon that premise, and it is essential to
understand the mechanism --- not just the effect --- because both
the inference cost measured in Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} and the
distillation of Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} follow directly from
it.

\emph{Training became conditioning.} An \emph{in-context learning}
(ICL) model is a pre-trained network that conditions on the training
set as if it were a \emph{prompt}, at prediction time, with no
gradient update on the target task. Concretely: the Transformer is
presented, in a single input, with the entire training set --- pairs
$(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)$ --- followed by the query row
$\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}}$; a single forward pass produces the
prediction, with the network's weights frozen. ``Training'' on the
task reduces to assembling the context; all the actual learning
happened earlier, during pre-training.
Figure~\ref{fig:icl-mecanismo} diagrams the two phases.

\emph{Where does the ability to ``learn'' without gradients come
from?} From pre-training. \emph{Prior-fitted networks} (PFNs) are
pre-trained on millions of small \emph{synthetic} datasets, sampled
from a prior over generating mechanisms \citep{muller2022pfn}; in
TabPFN, that prior is built from structural causal models (SCMs):
random causal graphs whose variables relate through randomly drawn
functions and noise terms \citep{hollmann2025}. On each
synthetic task, the network sees a labeled context and is trained to
predict the held-out labels; doing so at scale, it learns a generic
inference algorithm: the output of the forward pass approximates the
\emph{posterior predictive distribution} under the synthetic prior,
given the context \citep{muller2022pfn}; \citet{nagler2023}
complements this Bayesian reading with a frequentist analysis of
PFNs as pre-tuned but untrained predictors. \textbf{TabPFN}, in its
v2 \citep{hollmann2025} and v2.5 \citep{priorlabs2025} versions,
established the paradigm in practice, with a native context limit
(50{,}000 rows in v2.5) inherited from the quadratic cost of
attention.

One architectural detail of TabPFN is central to
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}: in regression, its output layer does
not return a number but a \emph{bar distribution} --- a discretized
histogram over the target's range, from which arbitrary quantiles of
the predictive distribution can be read. The model natively delivers
\emph{distributional} regression (Section~\ref{sec:fund-tabular}).
In contrast, \textbf{TabFM} \citep{tabfm2026,kong2026} --- an
independent implementation of the ICL paradigm, with a hybrid
architecture that combines attention between columns, compression of
each row into tokens and an ICL Transformer, and separate
checkpoints per task --- decodes a single scalar in regression: it
is an exclusively \emph{point} teacher. This asymmetry of output
heads, and not any difference in average accuracy, is what will make
TabPFN the natural teacher for the distributional distillation of
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}. Note also that both models carry
architectural limits inherited from pre-training --- TabFM, for
example, admits at most 10 classes in classification, was
pre-trained with contexts of up to 16{,}384 rows (larger tables
depend on length generalization or subsampling, as its own report
states), and as of this writing is documented in a technical report,
without a peer-reviewed paper \citep{tabfm2026,kong2026} --- limits
that become missing cells of the benchmark and require the
statistical policy declared in Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}.

The mechanism of Figure~\ref{fig:icl-mecanismo} has a structural
price: \emph{the training set travels with the model on every
prediction}. The training cost is close to zero --- assembling the
context --- but each inference pass reprocesses the entire context,
which puts the cost per prediction orders of magnitude above that of
a GBDT, whose inference walks a few trees. In inference-heavy
scenarios, that bill dominates any accuracy advantage ---
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} quantifies it and
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} proposes a way out.

Three terms follow from this mechanism and return in later chapters.
The model is said to operate \emph{zero-shot} because nothing in it
is adjusted to the task: neither weights nor hyperparameters. In
practice, the prediction does not come from a single pass: the model
is run several times on perturbed versions of the same context (in
TabPFN, column permutations and preprocessing variants) and the
outputs are averaged --- the \emph{inference ensemble}, whose size
multiplies the cost. And, since the cost grows with the context,
there are three levers to cheapen inference without changing
paradigm: shrink the ensemble, shrink the context (by subsampling)
or replace the model with a distilled student.
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} measures all three with the same
yardstick.

\section{Knowledge distillation}
\label{sec:fund-destilacao}

If an expensive model is too good to discard and too expensive to
serve, the classical answer is \emph{knowledge distillation}:
training a fast model (the \emph{student}) to imitate the outputs of
an expensive one (the \emph{teacher}). The idea originated as
\emph{model compression} \citep{bucila2006} --- a bulky ensemble
labels a large mass of unlabeled data, and a small network is
trained on those labels --- and acquired its modern formulation with
\citet{hinton2015}.

The central observation of \citet{hinton2015} is that the
\emph{probabilities} the teacher assigns to the wrong classes carry
information the true label does not have: when classifying the
fourth row of Table~\ref{tab:adult-exemplo}, a teacher that answers
``no, with 85\%'' teaches more than the bare label ``no'' --- it
encodes how ambiguous the case is. To expose that structure, the
entropy of the output is amplified with a \emph{temperature} $T$ in
the softmax,
\begin{equation}
  p_i(T) \;=\;
  \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)},
  \label{eq:temperatura}
\end{equation}
where $z_i$ are the logits; with $T > 1$, the small probabilities
stop being crushed. The student is trained on a combination of two
losses: the usual cross-entropy against the true labels and an
imitation loss against the teacher's \emph{soft targets} at the same
temperature. In regression, the analogous recipe replaces the label
with the teacher's predicted value --- and, in the distributional
extension of Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}, with the teacher's
entire \emph{quantile curve}.

The \emph{born-again} lineage showed early that the student need not
be a network: \citet{breiman1996} compress an ensemble of trees into
a single tree trained on the committee's predictions, with
surprisingly small loss of fidelity; \citet{vidal2020} give ensemble
compression a modern exact treatment. In the reverse direction,
\citet{frosst2017} distill a deep network into a soft decision tree
in search of interpretability, and the distillation of networks into
networks of equal capacity (\emph{born-again networks}) revealed
that even self-imitation can improve the student
\citep{furlanello2018}. The transversal lesson: distillation is
agnostic to the teacher--student pair; what is transferred is the
learned \emph{function}, exposed through the outputs. And the target
of the transfer need not be a point: distilling the teacher's
predictive \emph{distribution} has its own lineage --- from the
Bayesian posterior predictive compressed into a single network
\citep{korattikara2015} to the distribution of ensembles, in
classification \citep{malinin2020end2} and in regression via a
Normal--Wishart parameterization \citep{malinin2020rpn}, with the
same single-pass-inference motivation that drives uncertainty
distillation in vision \citep{shen2021} --- always, in that lineage,
with teachers of accessible weights or members and parametric neural
students.

With ICL teachers, distillation gains an additional attraction: it
removes exactly the constitutive bottleneck of the family (the
context that travels with every prediction), converting a teacher of
expensive inference into a student of cheap inference. \emph{Pocket
FM} \citep{pocketfm2026} establishes that agenda for
\emph{classification}, at scale, with a methodological safeguard
that Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} inherits: \emph{out-of-fold}
labeling --- the teacher labels each example conditioned only on the
other partitions, never on itself --- so that the student does not
learn from the teacher's leaks. What remains open in that line, and
constitutes this dissertation's contribution, is the extension to
\emph{distributional regression}: distilling not the mean but the
predictive distribution --- which requires the toolkit of the next
section. Chapter~\ref{chap:related} situates this framing in the
state of the art.

\section{Distributional regression: quantiles, CRPS and calibration}
\label{sec:fund-distribucional}

Predicting a number is useful; predicting a number \emph{with the
uncertainty around it} is what risk decisions demand. Consider a
model that appraises real estate: saying ``this property is worth
500{,}000'' and saying ``it is worth 500{,}000, and with 80\%
probability between 430{,}000 and 580{,}000'' are different products
--- the second allows pricing risk, setting margins and declining
decisions when the uncertainty is too large. Two models with the
same average point error can differ radically in the quality of that
second answer, and the point metrics (RMSE, MAE) are blind to the
difference. This section presents the instruments that see it ---
quantiles and the pinball loss, CRPS, calibration, and sharpness ---
at the depth Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} presupposes: here the
mathematics is deliberately detailed, because these are the
quantities in which the contribution is measured.

Before the distributions, the point yardstick. For a model that
returns $\hat y_i$ on each of the $n$ test rows, the three measures
used in this work are the root mean squared error,
$\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2}$, the
mean absolute error,
$\mathrm{MAE} = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i \lvert y_i - \hat y_i \rvert$,
both in the unit of the target, and the coefficient of
determination,
$R^2 = 1 - \sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 / \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2$,
scale-free. RMSE punishes large errors more; comparing RMSE and MAE
shows when the error is dominated by a few rows. Each loss has an
implicit target: the prediction that minimizes expected squared
error is the conditional \emph{mean}, and the one that minimizes
expected absolute error is the conditional \emph{median}. The
pinball loss, next, generalizes this second case from the median to
an arbitrary quantile.

\subsection{Quantiles and the pinball loss}

The conditional quantile $q_\tau(\mathbf{x})$ is the value below
which the target falls with probability $\tau$, given $\mathbf{x}$:
the quantile $\tau = 0.5$ is the conditional median; the quantiles
$0.1$ and $0.9$ delimit a central 80\% interval. Quantile
regression \citep{koenker1978} estimates $q_\tau$ directly, by
minimizing the \emph{pinball loss} (or quantile loss): with error
$u = y - \hat{q}_\tau$,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\tau(u) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \tau\, u, & u \geq 0 \quad \text{(underestimated the target)},\\[2pt]
    (\tau - 1)\, u, & u < 0 \quad \text{(overestimated the target)}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:pinball}
\end{equation}

The loss is a ramp with two slopes (Figure~\ref{fig:pinball}): for
$\tau = 0.9$, underestimating costs $0.9$ per unit of error and
overestimating costs only $0.1$. The optimal minimizer therefore
prefers to err upward --- and errs upward by exactly the right
amount: the prediction that minimizes the expected loss is precisely
the $0.9$ quantile of the conditional distribution. It is this
property --- the minimizer of the level-$\tau$ pinball loss is the
$\tau$ quantile --- that makes it the canonical instrument of
quantile regression; with $\tau = 0.5$, it reduces (up to a factor)
to the absolute error, whose minimizer is the median. Training a
model on a \emph{grid} of levels $\tau \in \{0.05, \ldots, 0.95\}$
produces a quantile curve --- a discretization of the entire
predictive distribution.

When the grid's levels are estimated independently, nothing
guarantees the monotonicity the definition requires --- the model
can produce $\hat{q}_{0.8} < \hat{q}_{0.7}$ for some row, the
so-called \emph{quantile crossing}, which makes the
``distribution'' internally inconsistent. The standard pragmatic
repair is to re-sort each row's predicted quantiles in increasing
order, an operation that never worsens the total pinball loss and
restores the curve's validity; it is the repair adopted in the
multi-quantile students of Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}.

There are, then, two ways to obtain a quantile model, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} uses both. Without a teacher, one
trains with the pinball loss on the observed targets: the model must
discover each quantile from a single value of $y$ per row. With a
teacher, the quantile comes ready-made: for each row and each level
$\tau$, the teacher provides the number
$q^{\text{teach}}_\tau(\mathbf{x}_i)$, and the student can learn it
by ordinary regression, with squared error, one output per level.
The two arms thus differ in the target \emph{and} in the loss --- an
asymmetry that Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} declares and mitigates
on the configuration side, re-tuning the hyperparameters of the two
arms separately (the effect of the loss difference itself is not
isolated).

\subsection{CRPS: a yardstick for distributions}

How to compare two predictive distributions? This dissertation's
yardstick is the \emph{continuous ranked probability score} (CRPS):
for a predictive distribution with cumulative distribution function
$F$ and an observed target $y$,
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{CRPS}(F, y) \;=\;
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
  \left( F(z) - \mathbf{1}\{z \geq y\} \right)^2 \mathrm{d}z,
  \label{eq:crps}
\end{equation}
the integrated squared distance between the predicted CDF and the
step CDF of the observed value. Two properties qualify it. First,
the CRPS is a \emph{proper scoring rule} \citep{gneiting2007}: its
expected value is minimized when the model reports the true
conditional distribution --- there is no way to gain score by
``bluffing'' a distribution different from what the model actually
believes, which makes it the honest criterion for evaluating
uncertainty. Second, it \emph{generalizes the absolute error}: if
$F$ collapses to a point (a point prediction), Eq.~\ref{eq:crps}
reduces to $|{\hat{y} - y}|$ --- so that the CRPS allows comparing,
on the same yardstick and in the unit of the target, point and
distributional models. Lower is better.

In practice, when the model delivers a grid of quantiles instead of
a continuous CDF, the CRPS is estimated as twice the average pinball
loss over the grid of levels \citep{gneiting2011},
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\operatorname{CRPS}}(F, y) \;=\;
  \frac{2}{|\mathcal{T}|} \sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{T}}
  \rho_\tau\!\left(y - \hat{q}_\tau\right),
  \label{eq:crps-grade}
\end{equation}
the standard estimator of probabilistic-forecasting practice --- and
the formal link between the two quantities of this section:
minimizing the pinball loss over the whole grid is, up to a
constant, optimizing the discretized CRPS. Being a discretization,
the estimator carries a grid-dependent bias;
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} reports the validation of that bias
under this dissertation's protocol and the reading rule that follows
from it (internal comparisons, never absolute values across works).

\subsection{Calibration and sharpness}

The CRPS summarizes distributional quality in one number; the
\emph{calibration} diagnostics decompose it. A nominal 80\%
prediction interval is \emph{calibrated} if it covers the target in
about 80\% of the test cases. The operational metric is the PICP
(\emph{prediction interval coverage probability}): the fraction of
test targets that fall inside the nominal interval --- a PICP80 far
below 80\% exposes an \emph{overconfident} model (intervals too
narrow); far above, an \emph{underconfident} one (intervals too
wide). Coverage, however, is a necessary and not sufficient
condition: an absurdly wide interval always covers and informs
nothing. This is why coverage is read together with
\emph{sharpness} --- the average width of the intervals: among
equally calibrated models, the one with the narrowest intervals is
the most informative \citep{gneiting2007calib}. The per-quantile
analogue is the \emph{reliability curve}: for each nominal level
$\tau$ of the grid, one measures the \emph{empirical coverage}
$\hat P(y \leq q_\tau)$ --- the fraction of test targets that fall
below the predicted $\tau$ quantile --- and plots the result against
$\tau$ itself. A perfectly calibrated model produces the diagonal
(the 0.2 quantile is exceeded by 80\% of the targets, the 0.8 by
20\%, and so on); curves above the diagonal expose quantiles that
are too high (overcoverage), below it, too low, and the S shape
around the diagonal is the signature of badly calibrated tails with
a correct center. Figure~\ref{fig:quantis-cobertura} makes the
mechanism concrete with real data from the distillation study
itself: in panel~(a), the prediction for \emph{one} row is a curve
of 19 quantiles --- 19 numbers in the unit of the target, one per
grid level --- and the observed target falls below the quantiles of
level $\tau \geq 0.65$; in panel~(b), repeating that count for all
rows produces the empirical coverage of each level, and the
resulting curve exhibits exactly the S shape described above (at
nominal level $0.20$ the coverage is $8.2\%$: tails too wide,
center nearly perfect). It is worth underlining what the horizontal
axis of these curves is \emph{not}: the levels $\tau$ are not
probabilities estimated by the model --- there is no sigmoid and no
classifier \texttt{predict\_proba} involved --- but the grid
levels, fixed by the evaluator \emph{before} any prediction; the
model answers with values $q_\tau$ in the unit of the target, and
probability only appears in the \emph{verification}, as the
frequency with which the test targets fall below each promised
quantile. It is the reading used in the evaluation of the
contribution (Figure~\ref{fig:destil-calibration}).

When the empirical coverage strays from the nominal one, there is a
post-hoc repair with a formal guarantee: \emph{conformal
calibration} (split-conformal) sets aside a calibration set,
measures the model's residuals on it and adjusts the interval
bounds by the empirical quantile of those residuals --- which
guarantees, under exchangeability of the data, marginal coverage at
the nominal level, whatever the underlying model \citep{vovk2005};
the quantile variant (CQR, \emph{conformalized quantile
regression}) applies the same adjustment directly to the predicted
quantiles \citep{romano2019}. Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} uses
this tool as a declared pilot.

\subsection{Two native distributional baselines}

Two classical models that produce predictive distributions without
any teacher close the toolkit --- the natural controls for any
proposal of distributional distillation. \emph{Quantile regression
forests} (QRF) extend random forests by storing, in each leaf, not
the mean but \emph{all} the training values that fall there; the
predictive distribution of a new row is the weighted empirical
distribution of those values, from which any quantile can be read
\citep{meinshausen2006}. \emph{NGBoost} follows the parametric
route: a gradient boosting whose outputs are the \emph{parameters}
of a distribution (for example, mean and variance of a Gaussian),
trained by natural gradients on a proper scoring rule
\citep{duan2020}. The parametric route also includes the LSS family
--- \emph{XGBoostLSS} and relatives \citep{marz2019} --- which
takes the same boosting to model all the parameters of the
distribution; NGBoost represents it in this work as its member
trained on a proper scoring rule. QRF represents the
non-parametric path; NGBoost, the parametric one --- and both
appear as competitors in Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}.

\section{Statistical comparison of algorithms}
\label{sec:fund-estatistica}

One last instrument remains: how to claim, with statistical honesty,
that one model is better than another --- or that two models tie ---
from results on a collection of datasets? This section presents the
theory of the tests that Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia} adopts as
protocol and Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} applies; the exposition
follows the canonical framework of the field
\citep{demsar2006,benavoli2017}.

\emph{Why not an ANOVA on the metrics?} Because the metrics are not
\emph{commensurable} across datasets: an AUC of $0.95$ on an easy
dataset and one of $0.75$ on a hard one are not on the same scale,
and means or variances across datasets mix apples and oranges ---
besides which normality, ANOVA's assumption, has no reason to hold.
The solution of \citet{demsar2006} is to work with \emph{ranks}: on
each dataset, the $k$ models are ordered from best ($1$) to worst
($k$); ranks, unlike metrics, are comparable across datasets by
construction.

A second decision precedes any test: what counts as an independent
observation? When each model is evaluated by cross-validation, it is
tempting to treat each fold as a replicate --- but the folds of the
same dataset share most of their examples and are not independent;
using them as replicates would artificially inflate the effective
$N$ and, with it, the apparent power of the tests
(\emph{pseudo-replication}). The correct unit is the
\emph{dataset}: each contributes a single number per model (in this
study, the measurement on the fixed hold-out), and the folds feed
only the description of within-dataset variability. All the tests
below presuppose this choice.

\subsection{Friedman: the omnibus test}

The Friedman test asks whether the differences between the average
ranks $R_j$ of the $k$ models over the $N$ datasets are explainable
by chance \citep{friedman1937}. Under the null hypothesis of
equivalence among all models, the statistic
\begin{equation}
  \chi^2_F \;=\; \frac{12N}{k(k+1)}
  \left[ \sum_{j=1}^{k} R_j^2 \;-\; \frac{k(k+1)^2}{4} \right]
  \label{eq:friedman}
\end{equation}
approximately follows a chi-squared distribution with $k-1$ degrees
of freedom. This approximation is conservative when $N$ is small
--- this dissertation's regime --- which is why the Iman--Davenport
correction is also reported,
\begin{equation}
  F_F \;=\; \frac{(N-1)\,\chi^2_F}{N(k-1) - \chi^2_F},
  \label{eq:iman-davenport}
\end{equation}
which follows an $F$ distribution and rejects with more power
\citep{imandavenport1980}. A significant Friedman says only that
\emph{some} difference exists; locating it is the job of the
post-hoc tests.

\subsection{Nemenyi and the critical difference diagram}

The Nemenyi post-hoc compares all pairs of models by their average
ranks, with built-in control of the family-wise error
\citep{nemenyi1963}: two models differ significantly if their
average ranks are further apart than the \emph{critical difference}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{CD} \;=\; q_\alpha \sqrt{\frac{k(k+1)}{6N}},
  \label{eq:cd}
\end{equation}
where $q_\alpha$ comes from the studentized range distribution
\citep{demsar2006}. The result is usually presented in a
\emph{critical difference diagram} (CD diagram), and it is worth
learning to read it, because Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} uses it
repeatedly: the models are placed on a horizontal axis at the
position of their average rank (better on one side, worse on the
other); a ruler indicates the length of the CD; and thick
horizontal bars connect groups of models whose average ranks are
less than the CD apart --- \emph{models joined by the same bar are
not statistically distinguishable} at that level. Two wrong
readings to avoid: the bar does not say the connected models are
equal (only that the test does not distinguish them --- absence of
evidence is not evidence of absence), and Eq.~\ref{eq:cd} shows
that the CD grows with $k$ and shrinks slowly with $N$: with many
models and few datasets, wide bars are the expected arithmetic
consequence, not an anomaly.

\subsection{Paired Wilcoxon with Holm correction}

For specific pairs of models, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is more
powerful than the Nemenyi, because it uses the \emph{magnitudes} of
the per-dataset differences, not only the global ranks
\citep{wilcoxon1945}. When testing many pairs, however, the chance
of false positives multiplies; Holm's procedure corrects this by
ordering the p-values and demanding progressively looser thresholds
--- family-wise error control less conservative than the simple
Bonferroni correction \citep{holm1979}. One arithmetic limitation
matters in this dissertation's regime: with $N$ datasets, the
smallest two-sided p-value the Wilcoxon can produce is
$2 \cdot (1/2^N)$ --- with $N = 5$, the floor is $0.0625$, and
rejecting at $0.05$ is \emph{mathematically impossible}, before any
correction. Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia} declares the protocol
consequences of this floor.

A simpler relative of the Wilcoxon deserves its own note, because
it is the instrument behind the headline result of
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}: the \textbf{sign test}
\citep{conover1999} ignores the magnitudes and asks only how many
of the $N$ paired differences are positive --- under the null
hypothesis of zero median, the number of positives follows a
Binomial$(N, 1/2)$, and the p-value is exact. The price of
simplicity is less power; the gain is total robustness to scale and
to outliers (a difference of $+0.001$ and one of $+1.0$ count the
same), which makes it the natural test when the differences live on
incommensurable scales across datasets --- exactly the case of the
retentions of Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}.

\subsection{The Bayesian test with a region of practical equivalence}

The frequentist framework has an uncomfortable asymmetry: it can
reject equality, but it cannot \emph{affirm} it ---
non-rejection is not evidence of equivalence, especially with small
$N$ and little power. \citet{benavoli2017} offer the Bayesian
complement. One fixes \emph{a priori}, by domain judgment, a
\emph{region of practical equivalence} (ROPE): an interval
$[-r, r]$ around zero within which the performance difference
between two models is considered irrelevant. The $N$ paired
differences, one per dataset, are treated as a sample from an
unknown distribution, on which a non-parametric prior is placed (a
Dirichlet process with a pseudo-observation at zero, of weight 0.6
in this study). The object of interest is the vector
$\theta = (\theta_{\text{left}}, \theta_{\text{ROPE}},
\theta_{\text{right}})$, summing to 1: the probabilities that the
typical difference between the two models (formally, the mean of
two drawn differences) falls to the left of the ROPE, inside it or
to its right. A high $\theta_{\text{ROPE}}$ means practical
equivalence; a high $\theta_{\text{right}}$, that the first model
is practically better. The posterior of $\theta$ is obtained by
sampling, and there are two ways to summarize it, which do not give
the same number: the \emph{expected mass} in each region,
$E[\theta_{\text{ROPE}}]$, read in this study with a 0.5 cutoff;
and the fraction of posterior samples in which
$\theta_{\text{ROPE}}$ is the largest of the three components,
which is the convention of \citet{benavoli2017}, usually read with
a 0.95 cutoff. Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} reports both. On
either, saying that two models are practically equivalent is a
positive, quantified claim --- not a non-result. One caveat applies
to this study's regime: the a priori pseudo-observation sits
inside the ROPE and, with small $N$, pulls the result toward
equivalence; the choice of the radius $r$ and the sensitivity
analysis are protocol decisions, specified in
Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}.

\subsection{Effect size and variance decomposition}

Significance does not measure relevance, and two complements close
the framework. Cohen's effect size in its paired variant,
$d_z = \bar{d} / s_d$ (mean of the per-dataset differences divided
by their standard deviation), quantifies \emph{how much} two models
differ on the scale of the differences' own variability. And the
correlation ratio
$\eta^2 = \mathit{SS}_{\text{between}} / \mathit{SS}_{\text{total}}$
--- here applied to the ranks --- decomposes the total variance
into parts: that attributable to the family, that attributable to
the model within the family, and a residual which, with one
observation per model$\times$dataset, bundles the
model$\times$dataset interaction and the noise, inseparable. A
high family $\eta^2$ would say that the family (GBDT, deep
learning, foundation model) determines performance; a high
residual, that the winner changes from dataset to dataset.
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} uses exactly this reading.

\subsection{Resampling and instance-level comparison}
\label{sec:fund-reamostragem}

Three instruments complete the framework. The \emph{bootstrap}
estimates the uncertainty of a statistic without assuming the shape
of its distribution: the data are resampled with replacement $B$
times, the statistic is recomputed on each replica, and the 95\%
confidence interval is taken as the interval between the 2.5 and
97.5 percentiles of the $B$ values (percentile bootstrap;
\citealp{efron1993}). What is resampled defines the question:
resampling \emph{datasets} gives the uncertainty of an average
across datasets; resampling a dataset's \emph{test rows} gives the
uncertainty of the result on that dataset. When two models are
evaluated on the same rows, the rows are resampled keeping the pair
(paired bootstrap), and the interval is that of the
\emph{difference}: if it does not contain zero, the difference is
distinguishable from the test's sampling noise.

McNemar's test \citep{mcnemar1947} compares two classifiers on the
same rows by looking only at the rows where they \emph{disagree}:
if the two were equivalent, each would be the sole correct one on
about half of the disagreements, and the number of exclusive
correct predictions of one of them would follow a Binomial with
parameter 1/2. It is the sign test applied to instances rather
than datasets. Finally, Spearman's rank correlation measures
whether two orderings agree (it is 1 for identical orders, $-1$
for reversed orders), without assuming a linear relation; it is
used whenever the question is whether a ranking is stable or
whether one quantity grows with another.

\section{Choosing a model: algorithm selection, regret and dominance}
\label{sec:fund-selecao}

The previous instruments compare models. What remains is the
vocabulary for \emph{choosing} one, which
Chapter~\ref{chap:framework} uses. The \emph{algorithm selection
problem} \citep{rice1976} asks whether it is possible to predict,
from a dataset's \emph{meta-features} --- properties measurable
before training any model, such as number of rows, number of
attributes and fraction of categorical attributes --- which
algorithm will perform best on it. A \emph{learned router} is a
model trained for that task: each dataset becomes a row, the
meta-features are the attributes and the label is the winner. The
alternative is a \emph{fixed policy}: always recommending the same
family, whatever the dataset.

Two notions make that comparison honest. The first is
\emph{leave-one-dataset-out} (LODO) validation: with $N$ datasets,
the router is trained on $N-1$ and evaluated on the one left out,
repeating for each --- the cross-validation in which the held-out
unit is the entire dataset, consistent with the unit of analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:fund-estatistica}. The second is \emph{regret}:
how much is lost by following a recommendation instead of choosing
that dataset's best model. So that datasets with metrics on
different scales can be aggregated, the regret is normalized
between the best and worst performance observed on the dataset ---
0 when the recommendation contains the best model, 1 when it
contains only the worst (the exact formula is in
Eq.~\ref{eq:regret-normalizado}, in the chapter that uses it). An
\emph{oracle} is the policy that, looking at the outcome, always
gets it right; it is not executable and serves only as a ceiling.

When there is more than one criterion --- accuracy and latency, for
example --- there is no single ``best''. A model \emph{dominates}
another (in the Pareto sense) if it is at least as good on all
criteria and strictly better on some; the \emph{Pareto frontier} is
the set of models that no other dominates.
Chapters~\ref{chap:benchmark} and~\ref{chap:destilacao} present
their multi-criteria results in this form.

\section{Chapter summary}

The chapter assembled four toolboxes: the families of models that
compete (GBDTs, deep networks, in-context-learning foundation
models), the distributional yardstick (quantiles, pinball, CRPS,
calibration) by which the distillation is judged, the statistical
framework that licenses claims of both difference and
equivalence, and the decision vocabulary (router, fixed policy,
LODO, regret, dominance) with which a benchmark becomes a
recommendation. With it in place, Chapter~\ref{chap:related} maps
the state of the art and demarcates, with dated checks, the gaps
that this dissertation's contributions occupy.
