\chapter{Distributional Distillation of Foundation Models}
\label{chap:destilacao}

Chapter~\ref{chap:framework} ended with a policy --- foundation model
first --- and an obstacle: the cost of serving it. This chapter
measures how much of that obstacle knowledge distillation removes on
the \emph{distributional} output of regression --- the framing with
no prior academic study (Section~\ref{sec:destil-novidade}) --- on 20
datasets. It is worth saying at the outset what the contribution is
\emph{not}: returning quantiles is something a GBDT already does on
its own --- this chapter's control is exactly that native quantile
GBDT. The question is whether the same student learns \emph{more}
from the teacher's distilled distribution than from the labels
themselves. There are four results. First, the teacher's point
accuracy does not transfer: the student trained on the teacher's
means beats the student trained on the true labels on only 4 of 20
datasets. Second, the shape of the predictive distribution transfers
in part: the student trained on the teacher's 19 quantile curves
beats its control on 16 of 20 datasets, with a median CRPS gain of
2.8\% (1.4\% when both arms are re-tuned), serving at about a hundred
microseconds per row. Third, that gain concentrates in the tails of
the distribution --- exactly where asymmetric-cost decisions operate.
Fourth, it is not universal, and the conditions under which it pays
off fit into a rule that can be checked before the student is trained,
by a single OOF labeling pass (minutes to a few hours, depending on
pool size and teacher latency).
Section~\ref{sec:destil-metodo} describes the method; the following
sections present the results, what threatens them, and when to use
each thing.

\section{Motivation and novelty check}
\label{sec:destil-motivacao}

\subsection{The price of the new leader}
\label{sec:destil-preco}

Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} established that the 2026 generation of
tabular foundation models shifts the historical top of accuracy --- a
consistent lead, without confirmation as a statistical separation;
Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}, that the fixed policy ``foundation
model first'' is regret-optimal on the primary metric, with median
regret 0.000 in the descriptive evaluation, over the 17 datasets in
which the family participates. The only obstacle to that policy is
the cost of serving --- and it is not an implementation detail but a
constitutive property of the paradigm: in-context-learning models
carry the training set as context on every prediction. Under a
standardized protocol on \texttt{adult}, we measured 7{,}416~\us/row
for TabPFN and 43{,}262~\us/row for TabFM, against 0.33~\us/row for
the tuned XGBoost --- factors of approximately $22{,}000\times$ and
$131{,}000\times$, respectively.
Section~\ref{sec:regret-ponderado} showed, moreover, that shifting
10--20\% of the weight from accuracy to latency (by median regret)
is enough to flip the
recommendation: reducing that cost is what decides whether the policy
can be used where latency counts. This chapter measures one of the
routes --- distillation --- in a specific framing: regression, with
TabPFN as the distributional teacher.

The classical tool for this problem is \textbf{knowledge
distillation} \citep{bucila2006,hinton2015}: training a fast student
on the outputs of an expensive teacher. For tabular foundation
models, the literature established it in \emph{classification}
\citep{pocketfm2026}; this chapter's question is what it can and
cannot transfer in \emph{regression} --- in particular in
distributional regression, where the teacher's output is not a
number but a complete predictive distribution, from which quantiles,
intervals, and calibrated uncertainty are read. Three questions
organize the study, echoing RQ3 of the Introduction: what
distillation transfers in tabular regression
(Sections~\ref{sec:destil-ponto} and~\ref{sec:destil-dist}); at what
serving cost (Section~\ref{sec:destil-fronteira}); and under which
verifiable conditions (Sections~\ref{sec:destil-oof}
to~\ref{sec:destil-regra}).

\subsection{Dated novelty check --- and the partial collision of October}
\label{sec:destil-novidade}

No novelty claim is made without a dated online check
(Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}); the complete record of the
searches --- scopes, dates, and works examined --- is in
Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares}. The picture they establish: the
distillation of tabular foundation models into fast students already
exists in \emph{classification} --- Pocket~FM \citep{pocketfm2026}
distills classification teachers into GBDT/MLP students over 153
datasets, retaining 96.5\% of the AUC with 38--860$\times$ speedups,
and establishes out-of-fold labeling as necessary; a sister work
extends the analysis to clinical data \citep{pocketfmhealth2026}.
Prior Labs sells a closed distillation engine alongside TabPFN-2.5
\citep{priorlabs2025}, with no public methodology or code;
TabDistill \citep{tabdistill2025} covers only the few-shot regime;
another line distills foundation models into generalized additive
models for interpretability \citep{tfmgam2026}; and GEAR
\citep{gear2026} adds synthetic expansion, for classification only.
On the acceleration-\emph{without}-distillation arm, TACO
\citep{taco2026} and CRUMB \citep{crumb2026} compress the context,
MotherNet \citep{mothernet2023} amortizes the fitting into a
hypernetwork, and TL-ANDI \citep{tlandi2026} transfers to
restricted-context tasks --- none compares the strategies against
each other on the same hold-outs. The underlying idea of distilling
predictive distributions is older and non-tabular --- from the
Bayesian predictive \citep{korattikara2015} to ensembles in
regression \citep{malinin2020rpn}, always with a teacher of
accessible weights, a parametric representation, and a neural
student (Section~\ref{sec:rel-destilacao}); what the sweep found
open is the instance with a black-box in-context-learning teacher, a
quantile target, and a GBDT student in tabular regression. From that
finding came three decisions: adopting Pocket~FM's out-of-fold
labeling as the methodological base; never claiming ``the first
distillation of a tabular foundation model''; and adjusting the
framing to what was open --- \textbf{distributional regression}
(distilling the distribution, not the mean, evaluating with CRPS and
coverage), the \textbf{three-strategy serving frontier}, and
TabFM as a teacher.

A contemporary work partially overlaps this framing:
\citet{jeong2026} distill TabICLv2 and TabPFN~v3 into neural and
tree students \emph{including regression} --- but only with point
targets (squared-error transfer of the teacher's means) and
evaluation restricted to RMSE, without CRPS, quantiles, calibration,
or a cost frontier. The reconciliation between the point gains they
report and our negative result is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:destil-ponto}: the pool-size sweep offers a
\emph{hypothesis} of moderation (residual gain in small pools), but
their recipe differs from ours on axes we did not replicate ---
labeling with full context, expansion with synthetic queries, and a
student trained only on teacher predictions --- so the
reconciliation is proposed, not demonstrated. TabFM's official
technical report \citep{kong2026}, still without peer review,
confirms at the primary source the architectural check of
Section~\ref{sec:destil-professores}. The distributional pillar and
the three-strategy frontier remain, up to the last search (November
2026; record in Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares}), without prior
academic work --- a public repository of September 2026 distills
TabFM into XGBoost for classification and point regression, without
touching the distributional framing.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:destil-metodo}

Three roles run through the chapter. The \textbf{teacher} is the
expensive foundation model, which returns a predictive distribution
for each row. The \textbf{distilled student} is an XGBoost trained
to reproduce the teacher's outputs instead of the observed labels.
The \textbf{control} is the same XGBoost, with the same
hyperparameters, trained the usual way, on the observed labels. The
comparison that matters is always distilled student against control:
their serving costs are of the same order of magnitude (within a
factor of ${\sim}2$ in the measured pairs), and the difference
between them is what the teacher added. The comparison against the teacher says how
much was left on the way.

\subsection{Teachers: one distributional, one point}
\label{sec:destil-professores}

\textbf{TabPFN v2.5} \citep{hollmann2025,priorlabs2025} is the
distributional teacher: its native API returns arbitrary quantiles
of the predictive distribution (the bar-distribution head: a
discretized histogram over the target's range), from which we
extract a grid of 19 levels ($\tau = 0.05, 0.10, \ldots, 0.95$). We
use v2.5, not the more recent TabPFN-3/3.5 releases
\citep{tabpfn3-2026,tabpfn35tr2026}, for comparability with the
version pinned in Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}'s benchmark and for
verified operation on the study's 16~GB GPU; the method uses nothing
version-specific, but the magnitude transferred may depend on it
(Section~\ref{sec:destil-limitacoes}).

\textbf{TabFM} \citep{tabfm2026} is an exclusively point teacher,
and that is a result, not a premise. We verified
\emph{architecturally} that its regression head decodes a single
scalar --- a check made on the code in July 2026 and since confirmed
at the primary source by the official technical report
\citep{kong2026}, not yet peer-reviewed --- and \emph{empirically}
that no usable distribution can be improvised from it: the spread of
its 8-member ensemble, used as a predictive distribution on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$, produces a CRPS
of 0.524 against the TabPFN teacher's 0.069, with 16.3\% coverage at
a nominal 80\%
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/\allowbreak tabfm\_\allowbreak spread.\allowbreak csv}).
Both teachers operate with an 8-member inference ensemble and a
context capped at 50{,}000 rows --- the same policies as the
benchmark; for TabFM, 8 members proved equivalent in accuracy
(measurement recorded in the versioned wrapper
\texttt{src/\allowbreak models/\allowbreak tabfm\_\allowbreak model.\allowbreak py})
to the library's default of 32, at a quarter of the cost
(measurement on \texttt{adult}).

\subsection{Out-of-fold labeling and student families}
\label{sec:destil-alunos}

The teacher predicts conditioned on the training set it receives as
context. If a row is in that context and we ask for its prediction,
it has already seen the answer: the prediction comes out too close
to the label, with too little uncertainty, and a student trained on
it inherits that undue confidence. For this reason the teacher's
targets for each row of the training pool come from an
\emph{out-of-fold} (OOF) scheme with $K{=}5$: the teacher
conditioned on 4/5 of the pool labels the held-out fifth, so that no
row is labeled by a teacher that had it in context --- in a
10{,}000-row dataset, each row is predicted by a teacher that saw
8{,}000 others and not it (Section~\ref{sec:destil-oof} measures
what happens when that caution is removed: accuracy does not worsen,
but the intervals become too narrow). The labels are cached once per
(dataset, teacher) pair and reused by all student variants --- the
pipeline's dominant cost is paid once. All students inherit the
tuned hyperparameters of the corresponding benchmark baseline:
capacity stays fixed. It must be said precisely what this control
equalizes and what it does not: between the quantile control and the
distilled student, the target \emph{and the loss function} change
(native pinball in the control; multi-output squared error on the
teacher's curves in the distilled one), and the inherited
hyperparameters were searched for squared error --- a configuration
asymmetry whose cost the re-tuning of both arms measures later
(Section~\ref{sec:destil-retune}): with both re-tuned, the sign
holds (16/20) and the magnitude drops from 2.8\% to 1.4\% median
CRPS gain --- Section~\ref{sec:destil-dist} presents the two
estimates together. There are three families:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{XGBoost point} \citep{chen2016}: trained on the true
    labels (control), on the teacher's OOF mean (\emph{soft}), or on
    a 0.8/0.2 mixture;
  \item \textbf{XGBoost multi-quantile}: the control variant uses
    the native pinball objective \citep{koenker1978} on the true
    labels; the distilled variant fits the teacher's 19 quantile
    curves as multi-output regression, with quantile crossings
    repaired by per-row sorting;
  \item \textbf{Quantile MLP}: the benchmark's MLP body with a
    19-output head; pinball on the labels (control) or squared error
    on the teacher's curves (distilled) --- symmetric to the XGBoost
    pair, to test whether the student's family matters.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:destil-pipeline} summarizes the pipeline and the
role of each variant: the true-label (\emph{hard}) variants are the
controls every distilled (\emph{soft}) variant must beat, and all
systems are evaluated on the same untouched 20\% hold-out.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:destil-metricas}

For point quality, RMSE and MAE. For distributional quality, the
\textbf{CRPS} (continuous ranked probability score
\citep{gneiting2007}: the proper scoring rule that generalizes the
absolute error of point predictions to complete predictive
distributions; lower is better), estimated as twice the mean pinball
loss over the 19-quantile grid \citep{gneiting2011} --- the standard
grid estimator of probabilistic-forecasting practice. We validated
this estimator against the Gaussian closed form: it carries a nearly
constant multiplicative overestimate of ${\sim}4$--5\% (ratio 1.048
in the four location$\times$scale scenarios;
\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/\allowbreak crps\_\allowbreak grid\_\allowbreak gaussian\_\allowbreak validation.\allowbreak csv}),
approximately independent of location and scale in the tested
scenarios, so all internal comparisons and retention ratios are
preserved --- but absolute CRPS values should not be compared to
other works'. For calibration, PICP80/90 (the fraction of test
targets inside the nominal 80\%/90\% interval) together with the
interval widths. The synthesis metric is the \textbf{CRPS gap
retention},
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ret} \;=\;
  \frac{\mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{hard}} - \mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{soft}}}
       {\mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{hard}} - \mathrm{CRPS}_{\mathrm{teacher}}},
\label{eq:destil-retencao}
\end{equation}
where 1 means the distilled student recovers the teacher's entire
advantage over its own true-label control, negative values mean
distillation hurt, and values above 1 mean the student ended up
better than the teacher itself --- what the networks literature
calls a \emph{born-again} student \citep{furlanello2018}. An example
with real numbers from Table~\ref{tab:destil-retencao} illustrates the
definition. On \texttt{cpu\_activity}, the control has CRPS 1.2943, the
teacher 1.0610, and the distilled student 1.1777: the teacher's
advantage is $1.2943-1.0610=0.2333$, the student gained
$1.2943-1.1777=0.1166$ (the $+0.117$ quoted later is this number
rounded), and the retention is $0.1166/0.2333=0.50$ --- the
student recovered half the advantage. On \texttt{wine\_quality}, the
distilled student (0.365) ended up \emph{worse} than the control
(0.328), with the teacher at 0.289: the numerator is negative and
the retention is $-0.97$. Since on all 20 datasets the teacher beats
the control (positive denominator), ``positive retention'' is
exactly equivalent to ``the distilled student beats the control''
--- and, being a ratio, retention oscillates when the denominator is
small, which is why the conclusions use the sign and the median, not
the mean. Latency is measured in \us/row on the benchmark's hold-out
(median of 5 passes for the students; a single timed pass for the
teachers, whose cost per pass is minutes).

\section{Two-stage registered design: hypotheses, criteria, and conduct}
\sectionmark{Two-stage registered design}
\label{sec:destil-desenho}

The experimental design was registered in \textbf{two stages}, and
the distinction matters for the confirmatory status of each number.
The first stage is the pre-registration of 2026-07-15
(\texttt{docs/}\allowbreak\texttt{desenho-experimental-destilacao.md}),
prior to any execution: the three falsifiable hypotheses H1--H3 on
the five central regression datasets, the post-pilot decision
criteria, the forecast of an extension of 8 to 10 datasets, and the
explicit commitment to report negative results with the same
prominence as positive ones. The three hypotheses:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1 (point).} The distilled student retains
    $\geq 50\%$ of the RMSE gap (teacher $-$ tuned baseline) on
    $\geq 3$ of the 5 central datasets, with latency
    $\leq 10$~\us/row.
  \item \textbf{H2 (distribution).} The distilled quantile student
    beats, in CRPS, the quantile student trained on the true labels
    (control), with interval coverage (PICP~80/90\%) within
    $\pm 5$~p.p.\ of nominal.
  \item \textbf{H3 (Pareto).} There exists a latency band (the
    pre-registration anticipated ${\lesssim}100$~\us/row) in which
    the distilled student is Pareto-dominant over the teacher with
    subsampled context.
\end{itemize}

The post-pilot criteria even anticipated the zero-retention scenario
(``the work becomes the negative result with a cause analysis'') ---
a commitment Section~\ref{sec:destil-ponto} honors for the point
axis. The execution deviations and the dataset-identity corrections
are recorded, with dates, in the design document and in
Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares};
Section~\ref{sec:destil-setup} and Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}
carry what affects the reading.

The \textbf{second stage} demands the same reporting rigor. The
headline's confirmatory instrument --- the sign test --- and the
extension's eligibility rule (CTR23 suite datasets with 3 to 100
thousand rows) were fixed \emph{after} an interim reading of the
picture on 15 datasets (11 positive retentions, sign $p=0.059$), and
the population was then completed to 20. A design with an interim
reading inflates the type~I error; treated as sequential in two
stages, the final result's $p$-value goes from 0.006 to
approximately \textbf{0.019}, and that is the confirmatory value ---
the stagewise-ordered adjustment is adopted over the smaller
Bonferroni bound (0.0118) because it respects the order in which the
two readings actually occurred, a conservative choice (the full
computation --- global error, extension alone, Bonferroni bound ---
is in Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares}).

\section{Point distillation does not transfer --- with either tested teacher}
\sectionmark{Point distillation does not transfer}
\label{sec:destil-ponto}

The first result is the negative the pre-registration committed to
reporting. On the 20 datasets, the point student trained on the
teacher's means beats its true-label control on only \textbf{4}; H1
is refuted already on the five central datasets. The natural
objection --- ``the teacher was not strong enough'' --- was tested
directly, replicating the experiment with TabFM, whose anchor gap
(the teacher's RMSE advantage over the shared control, the
retention's denominator) is nearly $6\times$ TabPFN's on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$: 0.028 against
0.005 RMSE. The picture does not change: only the two
\texttt{california} \emph{soft} cells beat the control, with a best
intra-pipeline retention of 0.11 (california/mixture), and the
remaining eight cells sit at the control's level or below. At full
pool, a tuned GBDT extracts as much point accuracy from the raw
labels as from the teacher's opinions about them.

Surprisingly, a residual gain appeared in the smaller exploratory
experiments --- and the contrast between preliminary smoke runs and
the pilot
suggested the hypothesis that the point gain is a low-data
phenomenon. We investigated with a pool-size sweep (caps of 800,
2{,}000, and 8{,}000 rows): there is a genuine low-data gain on
\texttt{wine\_quality} ($\Delta = +0.026$ at $n{=}800$), which
disappears from $n{=}2{,}000$ on and does \emph{not} replicate on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$. We therefore
attribute the residue to a dataset-dependent low-data moderator, and
report it as an observation, not a general effect.

That sweep is also the key to the reconciliation with the partial
collision of Section~\ref{sec:destil-novidade}: \citet{jeong2026}
report point-distillation gains in regression with newer teachers
(TabPFN~v3 and TabICLv2), squared-error transfer, and
RMSE-only evaluation, under a different protocol and suites --- and
with a recipe that differs from ours on axes we did not replicate
(full-context labeling, synthetic-query expansion, a student trained
only on teacher predictions). Our sweep offers a moderation
hypothesis for the contrast --- residual gain restricted to small
pools --- and our negative is scoped precisely: realistic data sizes
(datasets of 3{,}000 to 100{,}000 rows), student capacity fixed by
the benchmark's tuning. The two readings are compatible; the exact
reconciliation is proposed, not demonstrated.

\emph{Point distillation fails at realistic pool scale with both
tested teachers.} The distilled point student beats the true-label
control on only 4 of 20 datasets; the replication with a teacher
nearly $6\times$ stronger (anchor gap 0.028 vs.\ 0.005 RMSE on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$) does not change
the picture, and the pool sweep shows the residual gain is a
low-data phenomenon ($n \leq 800$ on \texttt{wine\_quality}; a
marginal $+0.005$ residue on \texttt{california}$^\dagger$ at
$n{=}2{,}000$) on some datasets only. H1 is
refuted; what the teacher knows about the \emph{mean}, the student
learns on its own from the data.

The distilled student beats its control on \textbf{16 of the 20}
datasets. If distillation had no effect at all, the distilled
student would beat the control on about half the datasets, as in a
sequence of 20 coin tosses; observing 16 or more wins in 20 has
probability 0.006 (one-sided sign test) --- corrected for the
design's interim reading, $p \approx 0.019$
(Section~\ref{sec:destil-desenho}). The sign test concludes only
this: the distilled student beats the control more often than
chance explains; it does not say by how much --- it counts a wide
win and a minimal-margin win the same way --- and it therefore comes
accompanied by the median and the per-dataset analysis. The median
retention is $+0.19$: on the typical dataset, the student recovers
about a fifth of the teacher's advantage, which corresponds to a
median CRPS 2.8\% lower than the control's (1.4\% once both arms are
re-tuned; Section~\ref{sec:destil-retune}). Two supplementary
instruments agree with different degrees of firmness: the one-sided
Wilcoxon gives $p=0.045$ (two-sided, 0.090: it would not reject),
and the Bayesian signed-rank analysis \citep{benavoli2017} assigns
probability 0.96 to the positive-retention region (ROPE $\pm0.05$;
expected posterior mass $E[\theta]=0.70$). The verdict therefore
rests on the sign test and the median;
Table~\ref{tab:destil-robustez} gathers all the variants.

\subsection{How large the effect is, and what it depends on}
\label{sec:destil-retune}

In the main design, distilled student and control use the same
hyperparameters, inherited from the XGBoost tuned in
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}'s benchmark. That keeps capacity equal
in both arms, but has a cost: those hyperparameters were searched
for squared error, which is the distilled student's loss and not the
control's (pinball). The control may be at a disadvantage by
configuration, not for lack of a teacher. To measure that, the two
arms were re-tuned separately (Optuna, 25 trials each, validation
within the pool) on the 20 datasets. The scoreboard does not change
--- 16 of 20 --- but the magnitude drops: the median retention goes
from 0.19 to 0.12, and the median CRPS gain over the control from
2.8\% to 1.4\% (the re-tuned mean retention is $-0.02$, pulled by
the negatives). About half of the measured CRPS gain therefore came
from the control's configuration. The number this work stands behind
is a median CRPS gain of 1.4\%--2.8\% over the control (a retention
of 12\%--19\% of the teacher's advantage), with the lower end as the
conservative estimate. An individual dataset's
retention changes with the tuning budget (it rises or holds on 9 of
20; falls on 11) and should not be quoted in isolation. Three facts
complete the reading: re-tuning changes the scoreboard's composition
without changing the count (\texttt{health\_\allowbreak insurance}
enters, \texttt{miami\_\allowbreak housing} leaves); distillation
and re-tuning \emph{compose} --- the distilled student also improves
when re-tuned, on 17 of 20 datasets; and the re-tuning's distilled
arm carries a weak asymmetry that \emph{favors} it (the Optuna
validation rows participated, via the teacher's context, in the OOF
targets), so the drop to 12\% is not an artifact against
distillation --- if anything, the true floor is even lower
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/\allowbreak retune\_\allowbreak analysis.csv}).

\subsection{How safe each of the 16 is}
\label{sec:destil-bootstrap}

The sign test counts as a win any positive difference, however
small. Resampling the test rows (B=10{,}000, students of the three
seeds), the 95\% interval of the CRPS gain lies entirely above zero
on \textbf{12} datasets; above zero by a minimal margin on
\textbf{1} (\texttt{online\_news}, gain $0.3\%$, CI $[0.0;\,0.6]$);
includes zero on \textbf{4} (\texttt{cps88wages},
\texttt{miami\_\allowbreak housing}, and \texttt{space\_ga} with a
positive point gain; \texttt{health\_\allowbreak insurance} with a
negative one); and lies entirely below zero on \textbf{3}
(\texttt{diamonds\_\allowbreak real},
\texttt{wine\_\allowbreak quality}, \texttt{fifa}). The 16 to 4
therefore decomposes into 12 clear wins, 1 win at the margin, 3 wins
indistinguishable from a tie, 1 defeat indistinguishable from a tie,
and 3 clear defeats --- Figure~\ref{fig:destil-forest} shows the
picture at once, and the interval column of
Table~\ref{tab:destil-retencao} carries the per-dataset detail
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/\allowbreak bootstrap\_\allowbreak lines.csv}).

The sensitivity that matters most is that of
\texttt{california\_housing}$^\dagger$ (binarized target;
Section~\ref{sec:met-datasets}): excluded, retention is positive on
15 of 19, with median $+0.17$, one-sided sign $p=0.010$, and a median

Wilcoxon moves to $p=0.067$ and stops rejecting at 5\%. The
qualitative conclusion does not depend on the dataset; the pair of
instruments ends with the sign test significant and the Wilcoxon
marginal. On that dataset, incidentally, the ``distributional gain''
is the calibration of a Bernoulli, not continuous regression: no
headline rests on it, and, wherever it reappears in examples, the
$^\dagger$ symbol accompanies it. Two complements close the
robustness: the bootstrap 95\% CI of the retention median is
$[0.08;\,0.44]$, and that of the fraction of positive datasets,
$[0.60;\,0.95]$

conservative test either, which is why that pair is judged by the
paired comparison (\texttt{friedman\_\allowbreak systems.csv}).

Three reading caveats complete the picture. First, retention is a
ratio and oscillates when the teacher's absolute advantage is small: the
\emph{born-again} case of \texttt{pumadyn32nh} rests on an advantage
below 0.001 CRPS (\texttt{online\_news}, at a 1.3\% relative
advantage, is analogous) --- which is why
the table carries the relative gain with its interval next to the
ratio. Second, the ``big success'' label must be read in relative terms:
the absolute improvement of $+0.160$ on \texttt{cps88wages} operates
on a CRPS of ${\sim}160$ --- a $0.1\%$ gain, indistinguishable from
zero in the bootstrap; the absolute successes that resist are
\texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction} ($+0.130$; $+2.9\%$, CI
$[2.6;\,3.2]$) and \texttt{cpu\_activity} ($+0.117$). Third,
near-null positives (\texttt{miami\_housing}, retention $+0.016$)
count as successes under the sign test --- the ROPE sweep (0.95--0.96
posterior probability between ROPE 0.02 and 0.10) delimits the
effect of treating them as equivalences.

\subsection{The four defeats, one by one}
\label{sec:destil-derrotas}

The failures are diagnosable --- but each by its own cause; there is
no common diagnosis. On \texttt{wine\_quality}, the cause is a
dataset artifact: 27.6\% of the test has an exact covariate twin in
the pool (the original dataset carries 1{,}179 duplicated rows), the
pinball control memorizes the twins and the student, trained on the
teacher's smoothed curves, does not --- on the rows \emph{without} a
twin the distilled student wins by $+3.0\%$ CRPS, and on the
deduplicated dataset ($n=5{,}318$, teacher re-labeled OOF) the
distilled student wins on all three seeds, with a gain of $+0.85\%$
and retention $+0.10$
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/\allowbreak wine\_\allowbreak dedup.csv});
the published ``defeat'' is duplicate memorization, not a
limitation of distillation, and it stays in the table because the
original dataset is the one in the registered population.
\texttt{health\_\allowbreak insurance} has \texttt{whrswk} as its
target --- hours worked per week, with 29.9\% zeros and
\emph{negative} skewness ($-0.34$) --- and its retention goes from $-0.26$
(inherited) to $+0.65$ (re-tuned): a configuration-sensitive
failure, not a scale effect. That leaves \texttt{fifa} and
\texttt{diamonds\_\allowbreak real} as the failures without a tested
explanation, on price-scale targets --- and, for them,
Section~\ref{sec:destil-regra} shows that the heavy-tail hypothesis
is not confirmed.

\subsection{Coverage: the clause of H2 that is not met}
\label{sec:destil-cobertura}

The quality transferred is not only the aggregate CRPS: calibration
accompanies --- and, on one criterion, exceeds --- the transfer, as
the reliability curves of Section~\ref{sec:destil-oof}
(Figure~\ref{fig:destil-calibration}) show in detail. H2's second
clause, however, receives here the explicit verdict that parity with
the refutations demands: the pre-registered requirement of coverage
within $\pm 5$~p.p.\ of nominal \textbf{is not met} by the pure
distilled student --- PICP80 falls inside the band on 10 of the 20
datasets (and on 2 of the 5 central ones) --- so H2 is confirmed
only on the CRPS clause; the remedy is measured in the conformal
pilot (Section~\ref{sec:destil-oof}), which fixes the coverage ---
on the criterion of the distance to nominal decreasing --- on 5/5
datasets in the distilled arm and 3/5 in the control, without moving
the CRPS.

\emph{In synthesis: the distilled student's CRPS is, at the median,
2.8\% lower than the control's (1.4\% with both arms re-tuned) ---
recovering 12\% to 19\% of the teacher's advantage. By point
estimate it wins on 16 of 20 datasets; by confidence interval, the
scoreboard is 12 clear wins, 1 win at the margin, 3 ties with a
positive point estimate, 1 tie with a negative one, and 3 clear
defeats.}

\section{The serving frontier: distill, compress, or shrink the ensemble}
\sectionmark{The serving frontier}
\label{sec:destil-fronteira}

Retention says what the student preserves; it does not say when the
student is the right choice against the engineering alternatives a
practitioner has at hand. To answer H3, we measured accuracy and
latency of \emph{all} the strategies on the same hold-outs, on three
positive-advantage datasets (one of them,
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$, has a binarized
target --- the numbers hold as a latency$\times$CRPS measurement,
but \texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction} is the frontier's
legitimate distributional showcase): TabPFN with the context
subsampled to \{1{,}000, 5{,}000, 10{,}000, 25{,}000, full\} (the
``compress the context'' arm, in the simple subsampling variant ---
CRUMB \citep{crumb2026} is that arm's representative method in the
literature); TabPFN with 1/4/8 ensemble members (the ``shrink the
ensemble'' arm); the distilled and control students (the
``distill'' arm); and the full-context TabFM as an accuracy anchor
(4{,}905--394{,}301~\us/row). Figure~\ref{fig:destil-pareto}
presents the resulting frontier: the latency axis is
\emph{logarithmic} --- equal horizontal distances are equal
multiplicative factors --- better is down and to the left, and what
matters is the vertical distance between the distilled student and
the teacher's curve at the nearest measured latency.

The figure separates three regimes. On the \textbf{point} plane (top
row), the true-label control student already occupies the fast
regime (2.6--5.3~\us/row) --- it is the refutation of H1 made
visible: there is nothing point distillation could add there. On the
\textbf{distributional} plane (bottom row), below
${\sim}250$~\us/row on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$ and
${\sim}1{,}300$~\us/row on \texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction},
the teacher-derived strategies leave the students uncontested, and
the distilled one is the CRPS-optimal among them (the native quantile
forest also inhabits that regime --- and on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$ beats it;
Section~\ref{sec:destil-e1}): CRPS 0.109 at 99~\us/row where the
teacher's cheapest configuration needs 245~\us/row for 0.094 ---
$2.5\times$ the student's latency --- on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$; and CRPS 4.43 at
169~\us/row where the cheapest teacher configuration that beats it
costs 4{,}576~\us/row --- $27\times$ the latency --- on
\texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction}.

The third dataset inverts the picture --- and the inversion is
instructive. On \texttt{kin8nm}, the teacher's \emph{absolute}
advantage is small ($+0.030$ CRPS; its relative advantage, 44\%, is
among the population's largest) and a \emph{single-member} TabPFN
delivers CRPS 0.040 at 150~\us/row --- essentially the student's
latency. There, the right lever is ensemble shrinking, not
distillation --- a \emph{latency} case, in which the teacher is
already cheap at the student's tier, rather than a small-advantage
case in the decision rule's relative metric
(Section~\ref{sec:destil-regra}): the cheap engineering menu settles
the matter before distillation enters the scene.

H3's verdict: \textbf{the pre-registered band is met on one dataset
and exceeded on the other}. On
\texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction} the distilled student is
Pareto-dominant in the strong sense --- better CRPS than the most
subsampled teacher (4.43 against 4.70) at $7.8\times$ less latency;
on \texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$ the band
exists, but it is the band below 245~\us{} in which no teacher
configuration operates (and which the native QRF occupies with a
better CRPS, as E1 would measure); on \texttt{kin8nm} the
hypothesis \emph{fails} --- ensemble shrinking delivers the
student's latency with a much better CRPS. H3 holds, therefore,
where no cheap teacher configuration reaches the student's latency
tier (\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$,
\texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction}); where one does, as on
\texttt{kin8nm}, cheap engineering replaces distillation --- a
latency condition, complementary to the decision rule's
relative-advantage gate. The pre-registered ${\lesssim}100$~\us/row
band is met at 99~\us/row on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$ and exceeded at
169~\us/row on \texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction}.

Two side findings have independent value. First, shrinking TabPFN's
ensemble from 8 to 1 member \emph{improved} the RMSE on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$ (0.2521 against
0.2555) at a seventh of the latency --- ensemble shrinking is an
underrated first lever, sometimes free. Second, the last doubling of
context (25{,}000 $\to$ 50{,}000) on
\texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction} requires the library's
memory-saving mode on the 16~GB GPU and costs $3.3\times$ the
latency (148.6 against 45.1~ms/row) for an RMSE gain of 0.05: on
GPUs of common size, the full-context teacher does not pay for
itself.

\emph{Where no cheap teacher configuration reaches the student's
latency tier, the distilled student is, among the teacher-derived
strategies, the CRPS-optimal distributional system below the
teacher's cheapest competitive configuration; where a single-member
teacher already reaches that tier, as on \texttt{kin8nm}, shrinking
the ensemble suffices --- and, before either, the classical native
of E1 should be tried.} On the frontier measured end to end, the cheapest teacher
configuration that beats the distilled student costs $2.5\times$ its
latency on \texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$ and
$27\times$ on \texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction}; on
\texttt{kin8nm}, a single-member teacher already
operates at the student's latency. Side findings: 1 TabPFN member
$\approx$ 8 members at 1/7 of the latency, and the last context
doubling is disproportionately expensive on 16~GB GPUs.

\section{Why label out-of-fold: calibration, not accuracy}
\sectionmark{Why label out-of-fold}
\label{sec:destil-oof}

The classification literature \citep{pocketfm2026} establishes that
in-sample teacher labels collapse toward the one-hot and must be
avoided. Regression, we show, behaves differently --- and the
difference only appears when the confounder is removed. Our
three-regime ablation compares: OOF (80\% context, no leakage),
in-sample with the \emph{same} 80\% context (with leakage, identical
context), and in-sample with full context. The design deconfounds
leakage from context size --- exactly what the usual OOF vs.\
in-sample comparison does not separate.

The result: leakage, isolated, does \emph{not} degrade accuracy ---
RMSE even improves on all four tested datasets, and CRPS on three of
the four (on \texttt{kin8nm} by 0.0006, a tie at the rounding of
Table~\ref{tab:app-abl-oof}; on \texttt{california}$^\dagger$ the
CRPS worsens)
--- and context size contributes little (though, on
\texttt{wine\_quality}, it removes a further 5.9~p.p.\ of coverage).
The damage concentrates on \textbf{interval coverage}: at identical
context, in-sample-labeled students lose 0.5 to 5.1~p.p.\ of PICP80
on all four datasets --- artifact
\texttt{ablation\_\allowbreak insample.\allowbreak csv} in
\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/}. The mechanism is
clear: the teacher is overconfident on the rows it has in context,
and that overconfidence --- invisible to accuracy metrics ---
transfers to the student as too-narrow intervals. In regression, OOF
labeling is a \emph{calibration} safeguard, not an accuracy one ---
a deconfounded refinement of the classification finding.

Figure~\ref{fig:destil-calibration} closes the calibration axis with
the reliability curves of the distributional systems --- promised in
Section~\ref{sec:destil-dist}: for each nominal level $\tau$, the
empirical fraction of targets below the predicted $\tau$ quantile,
with the diagonal as perfect calibration. The correct reading rule
--- the same as Figure~\ref{fig:quantis-cobertura}'s --- is per
level: a curve above the diagonal at some $\tau$ means the predicted
quantile is too \emph{high} at that level; the coverage of a central
interval $[q_{0.10}, q_{0.90}]$ is the \emph{difference} between the
fractions at the two levels, so what decides an interval's coverage
is the curve's \emph{slope} between its two levels, not its
position: steeper than the diagonal means too-wide intervals
(overcoverage), flatter means too-narrow ones (undercoverage), and
there is no shortcut ``above = overcovers'' valid for the whole
curve. On
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$, the true-label
control saturates in extreme overcoverage and the teacher exhibits
the S shape of badly calibrated tails, while the distilled student
is the closest to the diagonal; on \texttt{cpu\_activity}, the three
systems are well calibrated and the distilled one remains
competitive. The curve transfer with sort repair acts as a
calibration regularizer, not as a copy --- and a dedicated ablation
isolates the component: the repair improves or ties the CRPS on 20
of 20 datasets in both arms --- strict improvement on 20 of 20 in
the \emph{hard} arm and 18 of 20 in the \emph{soft} one (two exact
ties) --- but with a median effect below 1\%, so no conclusion of
the chapter depends on it (Appendix~\ref{sec:app-abl-sort}).

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/calibration_distill_en.pdf}
\caption[Reliability on the hold-out]{Reliability curves on the
hold-out (\texttt{california\_housing}$^\dagger$,
\texttt{cpu\_activity}): TabPFN teacher, distilled student, and
control; the axis mechanics is that of
Figure~\ref{fig:quantis-cobertura}.}
\label{fig:destil-calibration}
\end{figure}

\emph{Out-of-fold labeling protects the student's calibration, not
its accuracy.} At identical context, in-sample labels degrade
neither RMSE nor, on three of the four datasets, CRPS, but erode
interval coverage by 0.5 to
5.1~p.p.\ of PICP80 on the four tested datasets: the teacher's
in-context overconfidence transfers to the student. And distillation
even \emph{improves} calibration on one of the two criteria: on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$, the distilled
student has the smallest mean deviation from the diagonal among the
three systems --- but, on the central-interval coverage criterion,
it \emph{undercovers} (PICP80 of 0.596, against the teacher's
overcoverage of 0.951): ``better calibrated'' holds on the first
criterion, not the second, and it is the conformal step that closes
the coverage (with the $^\dagger$ caveat: there the target is
binarized, and ``calibration'' is that of a Bernoulli).

The symmetric objection remains: if the gain were \emph{only}
calibration, a cheap conformal correction on the true-label control
would reach it. A declared pilot (5 datasets, 3 seeds, per-quantile
split-conformal on both arms) answers that it does not:
conformalization fixes the coverage --- on the criterion of the
distance to nominal decreasing --- on 5/5 datasets in the
\emph{soft} arm and 3/5 in the \emph{hard} one (the exceptions: the
california-\emph{hard}, which already overcovered and whose coverage
conformalization moves away from nominal, with a PICP80 distance
from $8.06$ to $9.63$~p.p., and the cps88wages-\emph{hard}, which
was already within 1~p.p.\ of nominal and moves by less than
1~p.p.), but moves the CRPS by less than 1\% --- the distilled
student's shape advantage survives the correction intact.
Conformalization buys calibration; distillation buys shape
(Appendix~\ref{sec:app-pilot-conformal}).

\section{E1 --- classical distributional baselines: the strong native
put to the test}
\sectionmark{E1 --- classical distributional baselines}
\label{sec:destil-e1}

Up to here, classical distributional baselines outside the compared
families --- NGBoost \citep{duan2020} and quantile regression
forests (QRF) \citep{meinshausen2006} --- were not included: the
true-label quantile students play the role of the native
distributional baseline \emph{within} the tested families. The
limitation hides a possible confounder: if a strong classical native
closed on its own the gap we attribute to distillation, the whole
contribution would be oversized. This section closes that gap with
experiment E1, whose hypotheses and criteria were fixed in writing
before any result was read, with execution already started --- the
exact status is in Table~\ref{tab:destil-hipoteses} --- per the
study's conduct contract.

\subsection{Registered design (execution already started)}
\label{sec:destil-e1-desenho}

Two falsifiable hypotheses were fixed, with interpretation criteria
registered for \emph{both} outcomes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H-B1:} on the majority of the 20 datasets, the
    distilled student's CRPS is less than or equal to the best
    classical baseline's (one-sided sign test; $\alpha = 0.05$);
  \item \textbf{H-B2:} the classical baselines do not alter the
    frontier's reading --- their latencies are in microseconds (the
    students' tier), so they compete with the distilled student in
    accuracy, not in cost.
\end{itemize}

The criteria fixed in advance (gates): if H-B1 were confirmed, the decision
rule would gain the note ``a strong classical native does not
replace distillation where the teacher's advantage is large''; if
refuted, \emph{all} the distilled student's advantage claims would
be re-scoped in the papers and in this dissertation, with the
negative result reported with the same prominence. The protocol is
identical to the rest of the chapter's --- same 20 datasets, same
partitions (20\% hold-out, seed 42), 3 seeds, the same CRPS
estimator on the 19-quantile grid --- with one asymmetry declared in
the pre-registration itself: NGBoost (Normal distribution, early
stopping on a 10\%-of-pool validation) and the QRF (native quantiles
at the same 19~$\tau$) run with documented \emph{default}
hyperparameters, \emph{without} specific tuning, so the
interpretation is limited to ``strong default''. A same-day dated
addendum records the single execution decision: the QRF package does
not accept missing values (present in 3 CTR23 datasets; NGBoost
handles them natively), and median imputation fitted on the pool and
applied to the test was adopted, on the QRF path only --- a simple,
leakage-free, declared policy.

caveat that, against the \emph{best} of four teacher-free

($p=0.25$, one-sided sign test): it beats each cheap alternative
taken in isolation, not the best of them chosen per dataset. The decision rule thereby gains a
concrete instance and a complement. The step ``test a strong native
quantile student'' now names the QRF explicitly: it costs seconds of
training, serves at units or tens of microseconds per row and, on a
predictable minority of datasets, settles the problem before any
teacher needs to be loaded.

\emph{A strong classical distributional native does not replace
distillation --- but it should be tried first.} The distilled
student beats the best of NGBoost and QRF on 17 of 20 datasets
(one-sided sign test $p=0.0013$; one-sided Wilcoxon $p=0.0008$), at
latencies of the same tier
(classicals at 4--32~\us/row). The three upsets are diagnosable ---
on \texttt{wine\_quality}, where the gate already advised against
distilling, the default QRF beats even the teacher --- and they
convert the ``strong native'' step of the decision rule into a
concrete instruction: try the QRF first; it costs seconds.

\section{E3 --- decision utility: the distributional gain in problem units}
\sectionmark{E3 --- decision utility}
\label{sec:destil-e3}

The CRPS summarizes the whole distribution; a decision uses one
point of it. Whoever must reserve capacity to cover 90\% of cases
acts on the 0.90 quantile, and the cost of erring downward is nine
times that of erring upward: the expected cost of that decision is,
by construction, the pinball loss at level 0.90 (the newsvendor
problem). Experiment E3 therefore brings no evidence independent of
the CRPS --- it decomposes it per level $\tau$ and expresses it in
the unit of the target. Three systems are measured on the same 20
datasets and 3 seeds: the distilled student, the control, and the
output of someone who only has a point model (the point prediction
plus a single-deviation Gaussian residual, estimated on 10\% of the
pool and refit on the full pool). The two hypotheses and the focal
level $\tau{=}0.90$ were written before any reading of the result
(status in Table~\ref{tab:destil-hipoteses}).

The two registered hypotheses are confirmed. At $\tau{=}0.90$ ---
the typical service level of capital reserve or tail provisioning
--- the distilled student's realized cost is less than or equal to
the \emph{hard} control's on \textbf{18 of 20} datasets (one-sided
sign $p{=}0.0002$; supplementary Wilcoxon $p{=}0.0001$) and less
than or equal to the Gaussian workaround's on \textbf{17 of 20}
($p{=}0.0013$). The count exceeds that of the aggregate CRPS itself
(16 of 20) --- the tail edge is more consistent across datasets than
the average over all levels --- and comfortably survives the
exclusion of the binarized dataset$^\dagger$ (17/19, $p{=}0.0004$;
16/19, $p{=}0.0022$).

The pre-declared exploratory analysis (H-D3) explains why: the
profile of the gain per level $\tau$ is U-shaped. In the center of
the distribution ($\tau$ between 0.45 and 0.65), the median cost
reduction over the control is ${\sim}1\%$; in the tails, it rises to
$8.0\%$ at $\tau{=}0.90$, $8.2\%$ at $\tau{=}0.95$, and $8.7\%$ at
$\tau{=}0.05$ --- and, against the Gaussian, to $14$--$21\%$ at
$\tau{=}0.90$ and $\tau{=}0.05$ (13\% at $\tau{=}0.95$). The reading
closes the chapter's argument: \emph{the teacher's distributional
advantage lives in the tails --- exactly where asymmetric-cost
decisions live --- and it is there that the distilled student
delivers it, at about a hundred microseconds per row}
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/\allowbreak decision\_\allowbreak utility.\allowbreak csv};
analysis in
\texttt{decision\_\allowbreak utility\_\allowbreak analysis.\allowbreak csv}).

Three applied readings, with real rows from the OOF labeling
artifact (the same as Figure~\ref{fig:quantis-cobertura}'s), ground
what the identity between the cost ratio and the level~$\tau$ means
in practice. Three selection warnings first, because illustration is
not statistics: the curves are \emph{teacher} outputs under OOF
labeling (not the distilled student's --- the student learns exactly
these curves, with the retention Table~\ref{tab:destil-retencao}
measures); the individual rows were \emph{chosen} for legibility,
not drawn at random; and each choice comes declared with the whole
portfolio's statistic next to it. On \texttt{cps88wages}, an
income-cutoff policy at 500~dollars a week would approve by the
mean, comfortably, a row with a point prediction of 953; the same
row's 0.10 quantile is 338. The dataset has two rows with exactly
those covariates and exactly that curve --- one realized 356, the
other 1{,}132 --- and that is what a wide predictive distribution
\emph{means}: two individuals identical to the model, outcomes
$3.2\times$ apart, and an interval $[338;\,1{,}587]$ that covers
both while the point 953 misses both (the row is at the
${\sim}99$th percentile of the signed discrepancy, mean minus
realized; the twin is the counterexample the mean hides).

On the whole portfolio, with the \emph{same} cutoff of 500 on both
sides, 86.6\% of the rows the mean approves (12{,}247 of 14{,}141)
have a 0.10 quantile below the cutoff --- and the fraction depends
on the cutoff, which is why the full grid is reported: 48\% at 300,
74\% at 400, 95\% at 600, and 100\% from 700 dollars on. What the
rule buys is also stated from both sides: it is \emph{conservative}
--- it declines a lot, and the declined block realizes below 500 in
only 32\% of cases, slightly above the 29\% observed among all the
rows the mean approves ---
but what it \emph{keeps} is clean: only 9.6\% of the
q$_{0.10}$-approvals realize below the cutoff, three times less
than the base rate among the mean-approved rows. That is exactly what ``how much do you earn with 90\%
confidence?'' means: purity of the approved portfolio, paid in
volume --- a trade the single point does not even allow stating.

On \texttt{fps\_\allowbreak benchmark}, among the 326 configurations
with a point prediction in the 107--109~FPS band, the central 80\%
intervals range from 1.1 to 8.7~FPS --- the two configurations cited here are,
as declared, the two ends of that band: same mean, and the
minimum-performance guarantee (the 0.10 quantile) distinguishes what
the mean does not see (108 against 103). On
\texttt{video\_\allowbreak transcoding}, provisioning the reserve by
the point prediction overruns on 53.2\% of the jobs --- the mean is
close to the median, and half end up above by construction ---
while provisioning by the 0.90 quantile overruns on 5.2\%: below
the nominal 10\% (the teacher overcovers on this dataset), with a
\emph{median} slack --- the provision above the point prediction ---
nearly three times smaller than that of the
fixed margin with the same overrun rate (0.22 against 0.62) --- on
the
mean the order inverts (0.76 against 0.62), because the q$_{0.90}$
concentrates large slacks exactly on the volatile jobs, which is
where the fixed margin overruns.

The counterpoint closes the reading: where the cost of error is
symmetric and the decision consumes only the center of the
distribution, the U-profile's median gain of ${\sim}1\%$ does not
pay for the complexity --- the true-label student, simpler and
typically faster (on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$, the quantile
control serves at 50 against the distilled student's 99~\us/row,
and a point student is faster still: 2.6--5.3~\us/row across the
three frontier datasets), is the
right tool, and the rule of
Section~\ref{sec:destil-regra} exists to say so before any cost is
paid. Source:
\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/\allowbreak oof/\allowbreak tabpfn\_\allowbreak <dataset>.parquet}.

\section{When to distill: the decision rule}
\label{sec:destil-regra}

The rule condenses four results of the chapter --- the refutation
of point distillation (H1), the heavy-tail hypothesis not confirmed
(the log ablation, in this section), E1's upsets (on 3 of 20
datasets, a native QRF beats the distilled student), and the serving
frontier (H3) --- and this section tests the two questions that
close it:
does the student's family matter? does the heavy tail
explain the failures? (yes to the first; no to the second).

\textbf{Does the student's family matter?} Yes. XGBoost's pinball
objective, under this design's inherited hyperparameters, leaves
measurable room on several datasets ---
Section~\ref{sec:destil-e1-resultados} quantifies it: some
teacher-free alternative beats it on 9 of 20 --- and distillation
consistently fills part of that room; in part, that is the niche it
occupies. A true-label quantile-MLP student, by contrast, matches
the distilled XGBoost \emph{with no teacher at all} on small,
numeric datasets (CRPS 0.110 against 0.109 on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$; 0.047 against
0.064 on \texttt{kin8nm}) --- but collapses on
\texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction} (CRPS 9.66, with near-total
overcoverage), where distillation rescues it (CRPS $\to 4.91$). No
student family dominates; what distillation offers is robustness
across regimes
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/\allowbreak mlp\_\allowbreak students.\allowbreak csv}).

\textbf{Is the heavy tail the failure moderator?} No --- and the
correct reading is modest. We re-ran teacher and students under
$y' = \log(1+y)$ on the three defeat datasets with non-Gaussian
targets
--- \texttt{fifa} and \texttt{diamonds\_\allowbreak real}, on price
scales (positive skew), and \texttt{health\_\allowbreak insurance},
weekly hours with \emph{negative} skewness ($-0.34$) and
29.9\% zeros (its own diagnosis in
Section~\ref{sec:destil-derrotas}) --- plus two positive controls.
The transformation does \emph{not} rescue the failures
(\texttt{fifa} persists at $-1.69$) --- and the per-dataset picture,
with retention measured in the transformed space, is mixed:
retention rises marginally on the other two failures
(\texttt{diamonds} $-0.32 \to -0.03$; \texttt{health}
$-0.26 \to +0.04$, crossing zero) and moves the controls in opposite
directions (\texttt{kings} $+0.21 \to +0.04$; \texttt{miami}
$+0.02 \to +0.15$). The absolute CRPS differences
shrink to 0.004--0.025 in log space, but that is a change of the
metric's unit; on the dimensionless comparison --- the teacher's
\emph{relative} advantage over the control --- the transformation
does not reduce the advantage on the three defeat datasets (1.5\%
$\to$ 2.7\% on \texttt{fifa}; 4.9\% $\to$ 8.6\% on
\texttt{diamonds\_\allowbreak real}; 2.1\% $\to$ 4.2\% on
\texttt{health\_\allowbreak insurance}) and reduces it, without
eliminating it, on the two positive controls
($14.4\%\!\to\!9.1\%$ on \texttt{kings\_\allowbreak county};
$12.9\%\!\to\!11.1\%$ on \texttt{miami\_\allowbreak housing}). What
the ablation establishes is a weaker and still useful pair: the
transformation is free, it changes the error regime in which
teacher and students operate, and it does not eliminate the
teacher's relative advantage
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak distillation/\allowbreak ablation\_\allowbreak logtarget.\allowbreak csv}).

From four results --- the point refutation, the heavy-tail
hypothesis not confirmed, E1's upsets, and the serving frontier ---
comes the final
decision rule, the operational form of the answer to RQ3:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Before distilling: (i)~run cheap target transformations as
a diagnostic
(a log on price-scale targets is free, changes the error regime,
and how much it moves \emph{your} dataset is measured in minutes;
in the ablation, the directional effect is mixed and marginal ---
not a rescue);
(ii)~test a strong native distributional student --- a default QRF
costs seconds, and a quantile MLP covers the small, numeric
datasets. Distill when a genuine teacher advantage persists after
both --- a single OOF labeling measures that
advantage directly, at a cost of minutes to hours depending on pool
size and teacher latency (on \texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction},
80~thousand rows at ${\sim}149$~ms/row give ${\sim}3$~hours). Where
no cheap teacher configuration reaches
the student's latency tier (the two measured frontier cases),
distillation is the teacher-derived strategy that carries part of
the advantage below the teacher's cheapest competitive
configuration (a strong native such as the QRF occupies the same
latency regime and beats the distilled student on 3 of 20
datasets); where a single
teacher member already runs at the student's latency, ensemble
shrinking may settle the matter.}
\end{quote}

A declared descriptive analysis supports the rule's gate:
conditioning retention on the teacher's \emph{relative} advantage
(population median ${\approx}13.6\%$, oracle version), the half of
the datasets with advantage above the median has
positive retention on 10 of 10, and the four failures all
concentrate in the low-advantage half (one-sided Fisher exact
$p{=}0.043$; $p{=}0.033$ without the binarized
dataset$^\dagger$). The criterion predicts the effect's \emph{sign},
not its magnitude (non-significant Spearman), and carries two
declared limits: conditioner and outcome share the control's CRPS
on the same hold-out, and the conditioner uses the oracle version
of the criterion --- the OOF version, the one the rule recommends
to the practitioner, was not evaluated
(\texttt{retention\_\allowbreak conditional.csv}). A third limit is
structural: the rule is derived on the same 20 datasets on which it
is illustrated, with no out-of-sample test of the rule itself.

Each clause of the rule is a result of this chapter: clause (i) is
a heuristic salvaged from the refuted heavy-tail hypothesis (the
log ablation); (ii) is the MLP student plus E1; the OOF check is
Section~\ref{sec:destil-oof}; and the verdict's two ends are the
frontier of Section~\ref{sec:destil-fronteira} --- the
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$/\texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction}
cases on one side and \texttt{kin8nm} (a latency case) on the
other.

\emph{The decision rule --- transform, test a strong native, and
distill only the advantage that remains --- is verifiable in
minutes to hours, depending on the teacher.} The four defeats all fall in the half of the datasets
where the teacher's relative advantage is smaller (conditional
analysis above), but each has its own cause: duplicates on
\texttt{wine\_quality}, configuration sensitivity on
\texttt{health\_\allowbreak insurance}, and two defeats without a
tested explanation (\texttt{fifa},
\texttt{diamonds\_\allowbreak real}), which the log transformation
does not rescue (Section~\ref{sec:destil-regra}). The
precondition is measured with a single cheap OOF labeling, and E1
adds the intermediate step: the QRF as a strong native of
negligible cost.

\section{Discussion, limitations, and synthesis}
\label{sec:destil-discussao}

\subsection{What distillation transfers --- and what that says about
the teachers}
\label{sec:destil-transfere}

The consistent pattern across 20 datasets, two teachers, two student
families, and three ablations (the distributional evidence coming
from TabPFN-2.5 alone) is that \emph{point} information
saturates from the raw labels --- a tuned GBDT does not need the
teacher's opinions about the mean --- while \emph{distributional}
information (quantile structure, calibrated width) is carried by the
teacher and transferable. Not the mean: the \textbf{shape}. And the
shape is exactly the component a point-label student cannot learn
from the raw data to the same extent, and the most expensive
component to serve in context. It is worth making explicit,
therefore, what the contribution is \emph{not}: returning quantiles
a GBDT already does on its own --- this chapter's control is
precisely the native pinball objective, and E1 measures the
classical QRF and NGBoost. The contribution is the same quantile
student extracting from the \emph{teacher's} distilled distribution
what the scalar label does not contain: the shape of the
uncertainty, with the gain concentrated in the tails.

A practical consequence: leading in point accuracy does not imply
offering a predictive distribution. TabFM wins the accuracy tables
of Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}, but its regression head returns a
single value (a check confirmed by the official technical report
\citep{kong2026}) and the spread of its ensemble is no substitute
(16.3\% coverage at a nominal 80\%, measured on one dataset). Among
the measured teachers, TabPFN's distributional head is the decisive
architectural asset; the independent benchmarks that today evaluate
foundation models as conditional density estimators
\citep{izbicki2026,landsgesell2026} point the same way, and our
results quantify what the absence of that head costs. For the
practitioner with a 16~GB GPU, the order of the measured levers is:
shrink the ensemble first (sometimes free), compress the context
next, reserve the full teacher for batch processing --- and, when
distributional predictions must be served below the teacher's
cheapest competitive configuration, the distilled student is that
regime's CRPS-optimal occupant \emph{among the teacher-derived
strategies}; a native QRF also occupies it (and on
\texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing}$^\dagger$ reaches further
--- one of E1's three upsets), which is why the decision rule puts
``test a strong native student'' first
(Section~\ref{sec:destil-e1}). The complementary route we did not
measure --- post-hoc conformal calibration of the teacher itself
\citep{park2026} --- delivers calibrated intervals without a fast
student, but does not attack latency; FP8 quantization of attention
\citep{kubler2026} is a fourth emerging serving lever, also not
measured here.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:destil-limitacoes}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Heterogeneous, budget-dependent magnitude.}
    Retention varies from $-1.3$ to $+1.1$ across datasets; the
    Bayesian signed-rank analysis leaves ${\sim}25\%$ of
    \emph{expected} posterior mass on the negative-retention region
    ($E[\theta_{\text{neg}}]=0.25$) --- which does not contradict
    the 0.96 probability of Section~\ref{sec:destil-dist}, the
    probability that the \emph{positive} region is the posterior's
    most probable component; and
    Section~\ref{sec:destil-retune} shows that half
    of the main design's magnitude came from the control's
    configuration. What is delivered is the decision rule of
    Section~\ref{sec:destil-regra}, not a universal claim.
  \item \textbf{A population with one binarized-target dataset.}
    \texttt{california\_housing}$^\dagger$ remains in the registered
    population; every headline carries the sensitivity without it
    (Table~\ref{tab:destil-robustez}).
  \item \textbf{Four eligible datasets outside the population.} The
    eligibility rule reaches 19 CTR23 datasets; four stayed out
    (Section~\ref{sec:destil-setup}); the sensitivity with them is
    in Table~\ref{tab:destil-robustez}.
  \item \textbf{Classicals in default configuration.} NGBoost and
    QRF enter in documented default configuration, without specific
    tuning; E1's reading is ``strong default'', not ``classical
    tuned to the limit''.
  \item \textbf{Grid CRPS estimator.} The 19-quantile grid
    overestimates the exact CRPS by a nearly constant ${\sim}4$--5\%:
    internal comparisons preserved, absolute values not comparable
    across works; the 99-quantile grid ablation confirms the
    constancy on real data
    (\texttt{ablation\_\allowbreak densegrid.csv}).
  \item \textbf{One-sidedness and multiplicity.} The central
    $p$-values are one-sided, per the directional hypotheses of
    Table~\ref{tab:destil-hipoteses}; the headline survives the
    two-sided reading ($p=0.012$) and the sequential correction
    ($p\approx0.019$), but the supplementary Wilcoxon (two-sided
    $0.090$) and the post-hoc Fisher (two-sided $0.087$) would not
    reject. No correction is applied \emph{across} experiments, and
    the design defines no family for one. Under Holm, at the 5\%
    level, on the set of the three main sign tests (the headline,
    with its sequential correction; E1; E3), all three stand
    (adjusted $p \approx 0.019$; $0.0026$; $0.0006$). The
    supplementary instruments, already borderline on their own
    (one-sided Wilcoxon 0.045; Fisher 0.043), do not stand; and in a
    family that included them, the headline's adjusted $p$ would be
    0.057.
  \item \textbf{Group structure ignored by the i.i.d.\ split.} On
    datasets with groups (tracks by the same artist in
    \texttt{year\_\allowbreak prediction}, resales of the same
    property in \texttt{kings\_county}), absolute metrics inflate
    for all systems equally; the retention's denominator can
    distort, without affecting the direction of the paired
    comparisons.
  \item \textbf{Seeds.} The protocol uses 3 seeds per student; the
    10-seed re-run kept 16/20 and a median of 0.191 (against
    0.192), with a maximum individual shift of 0.013 --- the
    across-seed variance is negligible next to the across-dataset
    variance.
  \item \textbf{Hardware and teacher version.} Measurements on a
    single GPU delimit, not map, the hardware space; the results
    refer to TabPFN v2.5 and TabFM v1.0.1, pinned versions --- the
    method uses nothing specific to them, but the magnitude may
    depend on the version (Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusao}).
  \item \textbf{Regression only.} The distillation claims cover the
    distributional output of regression; classification distillation
    is established ground (Pocket~FM,
    Section~\ref{sec:rel-destilacao}) and is not re-measured here.
  \item \textbf{Teacher licenses.} Both teachers' weights carry
    non-commercial licenses, and TabPFN-2.5's forbids training,
    with its outputs, a model ``competitive'' with it. The results
    hold for research and evaluation; commercial use of a distilled
    student depends on a license from the vendor (the clauses' full
    text is in Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares}; the consolidation,
    in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusao}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Chapter summary}
\label{sec:destil-sintese}

The chapter delimits the space in which distilling tabular
foundation models for regression works. The point axis is negative
with both tested teachers, on datasets of 3{,}000 to 100{,}000 rows
and fixed student capacity: there is nothing to transfer. The
distributional axis --- the framing with no prior academic work,
with TabPFN-2.5 as the sole distributional teacher --- is positive
on 16 of 20 datasets (median retention of 12\% to 19\%: a median
CRPS gain of 2.8\% shrinking to 1.4\% when both arms are re-tuned),
at up to three orders of
magnitude less latency depending on the dataset ($7.4\times$ to
$880\times$ against the full-context teacher, computed from the
frontier measurements, \texttt{pareto.csv}; against its cheapest
competitive configuration, $2.5\times$ and $27\times$ in the two
measured frontier cases ---
Section~\ref{sec:destil-fronteira}), and
beats the best of QRF and NGBoost on 17 of 20 in the registered
E1 (12 of 20 against the best of four teacher-free alternatives
chosen per dataset).
% source: results/distillation/pareto.csv
The three-strategy frontier locates when each lever wins, the OOF
ablation shows that the right safeguard protects calibration, and
the negative results --- point refuted, heavy tail not confirmed,
partial redundancy with classicals --- convert into a decision rule
whose precondition can be checked before the student is trained.
It is the
dissertation's answer to RQ3: what the foundation model knows and
the baseline does not learn on its own is part of the
distribution's shape --- and it is that part, between an eighth and
a fifth of the teacher's advantage at the median, that distills.
With the three research questions answered, it remains for
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusao} to consolidate the program's
contributions, limitations, and future work.
