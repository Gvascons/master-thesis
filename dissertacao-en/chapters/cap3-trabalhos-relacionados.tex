\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:related}

This chapter positions the dissertation in the literature, walking
through six axes: the tabular benchmarks that consolidated the
comparison between GBDTs and deep learning, the evaluation of tabular
foundation models, the Kolmogorov--Arnold family applied to tabular
data, the acceleration and serving strategies for these models, the
distillation of tabular foundation models, and algorithm selection by
meta-learning. Each axis closes with the specific gap this work
occupies, and the chapter ends with a synthesis table linking each gap
to the chapter that attacks it. Since the field advances in cycles of
a few months, every characterization of third-party work comes from
dated online checks, and the scope qualifiers of the cited works
(``classification only'', ``no public methodology'', ``contemporary
with this work'') are deliberately preserved.

A note on method before the content. The literature covered by this
chapter moves in cycles of roughly three months --- TabPFN-2.5 in
November 2025, Pocket FM in May 2026, TabFM in June 2026, the
contemporary work of \citet{jeong2026} in October 2026 --- and a good
share of the central works has not yet undergone peer review. Any
claim of the form ``no work does X'' is therefore perishable. This
project's scientific-conduct contract (Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia})
requires novelty claims to be supported by dated online checks; this
study's searches span July to November 2026, and the complete record,
with dates and verdicts, is in Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares}. The
gaps stated below should be read as ``open as far as we checked, on
the declared dates'' --- never as timeless certainties.

\section{Tabular benchmarks and the GBDT vs.\ deep learning controversy}
\label{sec:rel-benchmarks}

The modern protocol for comparing gradient boosting and deep learning
on tabular data was established by two works. \citet{grinsztajn2022}
gave quantitative form to the saying ``trees win on tables'',
identifying the inductive biases that explain the advantage: the
irregularity of tabular target functions and the presence of
uninformative attributes favor tree-based methods over neural
networks. \citet{mcelfresh2023} nuanced the conclusion at a larger
scale of datasets: networks win with non-trivial frequency, and
dataset meta-features partially predict when that happens. Before
both, \citet{gorishniy2021} had standardized the comparison protocols
for tabular deep learning --- reference architectures, equalized
tuning, multiple seeds. More recently, TabArena \citep{tabarena2025}
maintains a ``living'' benchmark with dozens of datasets and a
continuously updated ranking as new models are released. The same
consolidation wave produced three resources we record for their
complementarity with our framing: the TALENT toolbox
\citep{talent2024} and its associated large study \citep{ye2024closer},
which standardize the evaluation of deep tabular methods at large
dataset scale; TabReD \citep{tabred2024}, which examines gaps of the
academic deep-tabular benchmarks --- showing that conclusions change
when the evaluation changes, the lesson convergent with ours; and the
result that even a nearest-neighbor baseline, revisited with modern
tooling (SGD, deep representations, stochastic neighbor sampling),
becomes competitive again \citep{modernnca2025} --- a reminder that
strong baselines are a precondition of any fair comparison.

Parallel to the benchmarks runs the literature on \emph{how} to
compare classifiers across multiple datasets. The canonical
frequentist protocol --- Friedman with corrected post-hoc, operating
on datasets and never on folds --- was fixed by \citet{demsar2006};
\citet{benavoli2017} argued, a decade later, that null-hypothesis
tests answer the wrong question (``is there a difference?'' instead
of ``does the difference matter?'') and proposed the Bayesian
alternative with a region of practical equivalence (ROPE). The
reference tabular benchmarks adopt the first framework; the Bayesian
layer, though available for nearly a decade, rarely appears in them
--- and the sensitivity of the conclusions to the ROPE radius is
almost never analyzed.

The two narratives --- ``trees win'' \citep{grinsztajn2022} and
``modern deep learning has caught up'' \citep{realmlp2024,tabm2025}
--- are often treated as contradictory. Our reading, supported by
the learning curves of Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}, is that both
are claims conditional on sample size and task structure, true in
different regimes: the leadership crossover between families is
real, dataset-specific, and measurable when the training size is
swept directly. The arrival of foundation models displaces the
entire controversy: the new axis is not tree vs.\ network, but
pre-trained vs.\ trained from scratch. It is worth recording that
the two reference benchmarks predate the second generation of
tabular foundation models --- not a methodological defect, but a
matter of timing: neither could have included models that did not
yet exist.

The gap we occupy on this axis is not one of breadth: our benchmark
does not compete there (it covers fewer datasets than TabArena);
it differs on three fronts that remained open. First, protocol depth
per cell: hyperparameter selection separated from measurement, with
uniform tuning per family, and frequentist \emph{and} Bayesian
inference --- the latter with the practical-equivalence layer via
ROPE \citep{benavoli2017} and a sensitivity analysis of the radius
--- on the same cells. Second, multi-criteria evaluation: inference
latency, training and tuning cost, robustness and interpretability
measured under the same protocol, not only accuracy. Third, verified
currency: simultaneous inclusion of the 2026 generation of
foundation models (TabFM, measured weeks after release) and of the
Kolmogorov--Arnold family, both absent from the neutral benchmarks
at the dates of our checks (record in
Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares}). Two exclusions from the model
roster are disclosed: the study compares individual models, not
model-combination AutoML systems such as AutoGluon
\citep{erickson2020}, whose ensembling layer answers a different
question from ours; and the releases after the version freeze
(TabICLv2, TabPFN-3/3.5) are excluded because of their release
dates, not by judgment.
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} executes this design.

\section{Tabular foundation models and their evaluation}
\label{sec:rel-tfm}

TabPFN v2 \citep{hollmann2025} and its v2.5 update
\citep{priorlabs2025} established in-context learning with
prior-fitted networks for tabular data: a network pre-trained on
synthetic datasets conditions on the training set as a prompt at
prediction time, with no gradient updates on the target task. The
line evolved quickly --- TabPFN-3 \citep{tabpfn3-2026} and, more
recently, TabPFN-3.5 \citep{tabpfn35tr2026} --- and inspired
variants exploring scale and
speed: TabICL \citep{tabicl2025} extends the paradigm to large
datasets; TabFlex \citep{tabflex2025} uses linear attention to scale
to millions of rows; MotherNet \citep{mothernet2023} amortizes the
fitting into a hypernetwork that emits the weights of an MLP in a
single forward pass.

TabFM \citep{tabfm2026} is an independent implementation of the same
paradigm by Google Research, with a hybrid architecture of attention
over columns and row compression, released with weights in June
2026. At the date of our integration (July 2026), there was no
paper: the citable sources were the official blog, the model card
and the repository. Since late September 2026 there is an official
technical report \citep{kong2026}, still without peer review ---
which, among other things, confirms in a primary source the scalar
regression head we had verified architecturally (a fact that matters
to Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}). The framing we adopt throughout
the dissertation is that of an \emph{inter-laboratory replication}
of the PFN/ICL paradigm --- Google vs.\ Prior Labs --- not that of a
second paradigm.

As for how these models have been evaluated, TabFM's public numbers
at release time came from the proponent itself (Google's evaluation
on TabArena), with only partial independent verification ---
standard practice in industrial releases, but one that leaves the
community without neutral evidence precisely in the period when
adoption decisions are made. On the TabPFN line, the independent
analysis of \citet{ye2025tabpfnv2} dissects the strengths of
TabPFN~v2 and capacity extensions --- an in-depth examination of a
single model, distinct from the neutral comparison between families
that was missing. In parallel, a recent line evaluates tabular
foundation models directly as conditional density estimators in
regression, with proper scoring rules
\citep{izbicki2026,landsgesell2026} --- distributional evaluation of
the \emph{teacher}, without any distillation or serving component.
That line consolidated over 2026: ScoringBench
\citep{scoringbench2026} evaluates foundation models with proper
scoring rules on 101 regression datasets and concludes that the
point and probabilistic rankings diverge --- independent
corroboration of the distinction that structures
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}; and a large-scale astronomical
application \citep{photoz2026} uses these models as probabilistic
regressors and explicitly points out that full-catalog deployment
may require distillation --- third-party demand for the
intersection this work occupies.

As far as we checked, there was no neutral TabPFN~$\times$~TabFM
comparison under an identical protocol --- same partitions, same
preprocessing, same context-subsampling policy. The demand is not
bureaucratic: in a field where models receive monthly updates and
published results mix versions, a comparison is only interpretable
with pinned versions and mirrored policies (in our case, the same
50{,}000-row context cap and the same structural exclusion of
the 100-class dataset for both models).
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} provides that comparison, framed as an
inter-laboratory replication of the paradigm; and the
distributional evaluation of the \emph{teachers} --- complementary
to the conditional-density benchmarks cited --- reappears in
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} as input to the distillation, an axis
none of the evaluation works covers.

\section{The Kolmogorov--Arnold family on tabular data}
\label{sec:rel-kan}

On the proponents' side, Kolmogorov--Arnold networks (KAN)
\citep{kan2024} invert the MLP: learnable univariate activation
functions (B-splines) live on the edges, and the nodes merely sum
--- an inductive bias of smooth additivity. The work had a
high-impact reception (an oral at ICLR 2025), but its original
evidence concentrates on symbolic regression and scientific
problems, not heterogeneous tabular data. The most direct tabular
extension is TabKAN \citep{tabkan2025}, a modular framework of KAN
variants whose best reported configuration (ChebyKAN) claims to beat
XGBoost and tabular transformers on ten datasets --- average AUC of
0.857 against XGBoost's 0.814 on the eight binary ones. There is
also TabKANet \citep{tabkanet2024}, which uses KAN layers only as a
numeric-embedding module inside a transformer.

The critical literature points in the opposite direction from the
proponents. \citet{kancritical2024} compared KAN and MLP controlling
either the number of parameters or the FLOPs, and found the MLP
winning on six of eight machine-learning tasks, with KAN dominating
only in symbolic regression. \citet{poeta2024} conducted the first
independent KAN-vs-MLP benchmark on tabular data, with results
favorable to KAN in accuracy but at higher computational cost; it
is a pioneering study and its choices are defensible for a first
investigation, but two scope limitations matter here --- there are
no GBDT baselines and training uses only ten epochs --- so the
study does not answer the question the practitioner asks (``does
KAN compete with XGBoost?''). As for TabKAN, three limitations of
the evidence matter here: six of the ten datasets have fewer than
8{,}000 samples, there is no regression, and the XGBoost baseline
used for comparison appears undertuned (an AUC of 0.726 on \texttt{credit\_g},
when our own tuning obtains ${\approx}0.77$ on the same dataset ---
0.767 on the hold-out, 0.786 in cross-validation).

This study's claim is therefore that of the \emph{first test of the
family against the full grid of families under a multi-criteria
protocol}: \citet{poeta2024} is earlier in the isolated comparison,
and the claim was scoped around it (the dated record of the
correction is in Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares}).

What remained open --- and remained open, outside this work, up to
the last recorded search --- is the test of the Kolmogorov--Arnold
family against the full GBDT/DL/foundation-model slate in a
uniform-protocol multi-criteria benchmark: \citet{poeta2024} have
no GBDTs, \citet{kancritical2024} have neither GBDTs nor foundation
models, and neither measures cost axes. No large-scale neutral
tabular benchmark included KANs at the checked dates.
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} provides exactly that test, with
sanity checks against the proponents' published values; the outcome
is unfavorable to the strong claims --- KAN and TabKAN finish in
the bottom third on all three tasks, with TabKAN behind TabNet
itself in multiclass and regression --- in line with the critical
literature and consistent with the hypothesis of undertuned
baselines in the original papers, with the caveat, disclosed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}, that TabKAN is our own
reimplementation (AdamW with mini-batches in place of the original
L-BFGS) and KAN had its normalization and grid range adapted: the
conclusion holds for this grid's protocol.

\section{Acceleration and serving of tabular foundation models}
\label{sec:rel-serving}

The main operational weakness of tabular foundation models is
inference cost: every prediction re-conditions on the entire
training set. A \emph{distillation-free} acceleration literature
grew rapidly in 2026, and it is the direct competitor of the
strategy we study in Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}.

TACO \citep{taco2026} learns, end to end, a compression of the
in-context set to about 1\% of its original size, with inference up
to 94$\times$ faster. CRUMB \citep{crumb2026} selects a compact
context by distributional matching (MMD), without retraining the
model --- it is the representative method of the ``compress the
context'' arm that we use as a conceptual reference (our frontier
measures the simplest variant, by subsampling). In the same general
direction, there are proposals for memory-efficient tabular
foundation models \citep{memeff2026}. The arm kept growing at a
monthly pace: between September and October 2026 came the
replacement of up to 96\% of the transformer's blocks by a
closed-form affine translator \citep{shallow2026}, activation
alignment to operate with reduced contexts \citep{actalign2026},
and the \emph{Fast} variant of TabPFN-3.5
\citep{tabpfn35tr2026} --- all, as far as we
checked, evaluated only on classification or point prediction: the
TabPFN-3.5 report evaluates distributionally (on ScoringBench) only
the full model, not the \emph{Fast} variant --- and distributional
evaluation \emph{of the teacher} is still not distillation. This
work's distributional gap is preserved.

In the direction of amortization and transfer, MotherNet
\citep{mothernet2023} amortizes the fitting into a hypernetwork
that emits an MLP's weights in a single forward pass --- it
eliminates the re-conditioning cost by trading it for a generator
network; MotherTree \citep{mothertree2026} takes the same idea to a
symbolic student, emitting a standalone decision tree --- fast
predictors with neither teacher nor distillation, conceptual
neighbors that attest to the demand for sub-millisecond prediction.
TL-ANDI \citep{tlandi2026} combines distilled labels and anchoring
for transfer to restricted-context tasks --- a goal orthogonal to
ours (it neither produces fast students nor addresses the
distributional axis), cited for completeness of the map.

FP8 quantization of attention \citep{kubler2026} is a fourth
emerging serving lever, which we do not measure in this work. And
post-hoc conformal calibration of the \emph{teacher} itself
\citep{park2026} is the complementary route to calibrated intervals
without any fast student --- one pays the foundation model's
latency in exchange for coverage guarantees.

Finally, there is plain engineering. Truncating the context,
shrinking the inference ensemble and caching the processed context
are current practice --- and, as far as we checked, folklore: the
works above rarely compare their proposals against these cheap
levers, and the cheap levers are almost never measured against each
other.

The gap here is one of cross-comparison: each cited work evaluates
its own strategy against the full \emph{teacher}; none measures the
strategies \emph{against each other} --- distill vs.\ compress the
context vs.\ shrink the ensemble --- on the same hold-out sets,
under the same protocol and with the same cost axes. That crossed
frontier is one of the contributions of
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}, which executes it and extracts two
findings of its own: ensemble shrinking is underrated (one member
approximates eight at a seventh of the latency) and the last
doubling of context is disproportionately expensive on 16~GB GPUs.

\section{Distillation of tabular foundation models}
\label{sec:rel-destilacao}

The mechanics of training a small model on the outputs of a large
one is established literature: model compression via soft labels
and transfer sets goes back to \citet{bucila2006}, the modern
formulation with temperature is due to \citet{hinton2015}, and the
transposition to trees has its own lineage --- \citet{breiman1996},
the born-again trees of \citet{vidal2020} and the distillation into
soft decision trees of \citet{frosst2017}. This is the machinery we
start from; what changes with foundation models is the
\emph{teacher} (an in-context-learning model, whose cost is at
inference) and, in our case, the target (quantile curves, not
labels).

This machinery, it is worth recording, is enjoying a renaissance
outside the tabular world: distillation is a central piece of the
post-training recipes of contemporary large language models, in
three variants --- the classical \emph{off-policy} one, from a
larger teacher to a smaller student, whether through distributions
(soft labels, as in the pre-training of the Gemma family,
\citealp{gemma2025}) or through generated text (hard labels, as in
the distilled models of DeepSeek-R1, \citealp{deepseek2025}); the
\emph{on-policy} one, in which the teacher corrects the student's
own generations \citep{agarwal2024}; and self-distillation, in
which the teacher is the model itself in a privileged
condition\footnote{A practitioner's overview of these three
variants in the frontier models of 2026 is at
\url{https://huggingface.co/blog/sergiopaniego/distillation-2026}
(accessed 2026-11-05).}. This dissertation's contribution is an
instance of the first variant --- expensive teacher, cheap student,
soft labels --- transposed to the tabular world with one difference
of target: what is transferred is not a distribution over tokens or
classes, but the predictive distribution of a regression,
represented by quantiles. None of these language works touches
tabular data; the parallel serves as context, not competition.

The work that establishes the distillation of tabular foundation
models is Pocket FM \citep{pocketfm2026}:
XGBoost/CatBoost/LightGBM/MLP students trained on soft labels from
multiple teachers (TabICLv2, TabPFNv2.6, LimiX, Orion-MSP) over 153
datasets, retaining 96.5\% of the teacher's AUC (a specific
configuration, not an average) with speedups of
38--860$\times$. Two methodological contributions of Pocket FM
matter directly to this dissertation: the demonstration that
stratified \emph{out-of-fold} (OOF) labeling is necessary --- an
in-context-learning teacher that scores its own training set
produces nearly deterministic labels, collapsing the soft targets
--- and the analysis of when distillation works or fails (gains
concentrated at low dimensionality). The clinical sister paper
\citep{pocketfmhealth2026} extends the analysis to 19 health
datasets, adding calibration and fairness, and reports that
multiple teachers do not beat the best single teacher. Both works
are \textbf{classification only}. We adopt Pocket FM's OOF labeling
as a basis --- reinventing it would be worse, ignoring it would be
leakage --- and we delimit our contribution on that specific axis
to what OOF protects \emph{in distributional regression}:
calibration, not accuracy (Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}).

Around this line orbit variants and neighbors. GEAR \citep{gear2026}
adds a two-stage variant with synthetic expansion of the transfer
set --- classification only; in the same family, \citet{dey2026}
distill a TabPFN teacher into a compact feed-forward network
(${\sim}6{,}000$ times smaller) for credit decisioning,
evaluated on accuracy and AUC --- also classification only.
TabDistill \citep{tabdistill2025} distills transformers into tiny
MLPs in the few-shot classification regime (and occupies the name,
which is why we do not use it). Another line distills foundation
models into generalized additive models (GAMs) for
interpretability, not speed \citep{tfmgam2026}. And Prior Labs
sells, alongside TabPFN-2.5, a closed distillation engine
\citep{priorlabs2025}: proprietary, with accuracy--latency
comparisons in the technical report but \textbf{no public
methodology or code} --- evidence of industrial demand for the
strategy, not scientific evidence about it.

\emph{Outside the tabular world, distilling distributions is an
established line.} The Bayesian posterior predictive has been
distilled into a single network since \citet{korattikara2015}; the
distribution of ensembles is distilled in classification
\citep{malinin2020end2} and in regression via a Normal--Wishart
parameterization \citep{malinin2020rpn}; uncertainty distillation
for single-pass inference is active in vision \citep{shen2021};
and a recent work distills the uncertainty of a deep ensemble in
tabular regression evaluating CRPS and coverage
\citep{park2025uncertainty}. All that lineage, however,
presupposes a teacher with accessible weights or members, a
parametric (Gaussian, Dirichlet, Normal--Wishart) or sample-based
representation, and a neural student. In the pre-TFM tabular
world, distillation into boosted trees covers regression only at
the point level \citep{fakoor2020}. None of these works has a
black-box in-context-learning teacher, a non-parametric quantile
target or a multi-quantile GBDT student --- the cell this work
occupies.

\emph{The distributional pieces exist --- separately.} No isolated
ingredient of distributional distillation is new, and the gap lies
precisely in the composition. Quantile regression with the pinball
loss --- the asymmetric loss whose minimizer is the $\tau$ quantile
--- is classical \citep{koenker1978}; the CRPS as a proper scoring
rule and its standard estimator on a quantile grid are standard in
probabilistic forecasting \citep{gneiting2007,gneiting2011}; and
XGBoost \citep{chen2016} supports, since version 2.0, native
multi-quantile objectives --- which makes a quantile GBDT a natural
student body for receiving quantile curves. On the \emph{teacher}
side, TabPFN v2.5 exposes arbitrary quantiles of the predictive
distribution through its native API. The pieces were all on the
table; no academic work had connected them, for a tabular
foundation-model teacher, in a distillation protocol evaluated by
CRPS and coverage (and no public repository,
in the distributional framing --- the September 2026 one that
distills TabFM into XGBoost stays at the point
level\footnote{TabFMKD,
\url{https://github.com/gegen07/TabFMKD} (last commit 2026-09-14;
accessed 2026-11-23): OOF soft labels for classification and a
convex teacher--label combination for point regression.}).

Contemporary with this work (2026-10-01, when our experiments were
already concluded), \citet{jeong2026} extend distillation, on the
regression side, to \textbf{point targets}: squared-error transfer
of the teacher's means, evaluated on RMSE (their classification
experiments use soft cross-entropy and AUC/log-loss), with stronger
teachers (TabPFN~v3 and TabICLv2) and a different protocol,
reporting point gains. Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} discusses the
reconciliation between those gains and our scoped negative result
--- the plausible moderator is the low-data regime, which our
training-size sweep scans directly, compounded by the difference
in teacher and protocol. The positioning point remains:
\citet{jeong2026} touch neither quantiles, nor CRPS, nor
calibration, nor a strategy frontier.

In the searches conducted between July and November 2026 (protocol,
expressions and dates in Appendix~\ref{app:suplementares}), we
found no public work covering, \textbf{for tabular foundation
models}: (i) \emph{distributional} distillation in regression ---
transfer of quantile curves, evaluated by CRPS and interval
coverage; (ii) a frontier between serving strategies measured on
the same hold-out sets. A third point is narrower: TabFM already
appears as a teacher in the September 2026 public repository cited
above, restricted to classification and point regression; what
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} adds is the test of TabFM as a
\emph{distributional} teacher, and the result is an architectural
negative --- TabFM's regression head is point-valued (confirmed by
the official technical report, \citealp{kong2026}), and the spread
of its ensemble is measurably useless as a predictive
distribution. Finally, we record what we do \emph{not} claim: the
basic formulation ``distill a tabular foundation model into a fast
student'' belongs to Pocket FM, and under no circumstances do we
claim the first distillation of tabular foundation models.

\section{Algorithm selection and meta-learning}
\label{sec:rel-selecao}

Recommending an algorithm from meta-features of the problem goes
back to the algorithm selection problem of \citet{rice1976} and has
a long meta-learning literature. The result of
\citet{mcelfresh2023} --- that meta-features partially predict when
networks beat trees --- is the modern version of that promise. The
historical experience, however, is of fragility outside large
dataset collections: with few observations, learned routers
overfit. Independent contemporary evidence points the same way in
the foundation-model era: global meta-feature approaches do not
prove robust for explaining or selecting among models of that
generation \citep{herre2026}.

What practice lacks is not yet another recommender, but the honest
validation of the recommender in the regime where real benchmarks
operate: a small $N$ of datasets. With $N{=}18$, honesty demands
explicit leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) validation --- each dataset
predicted by a model trained on the others --- which the
recommendation literature rarely reports in this regime. Our LODO
analysis (Chapter~\ref{chap:framework}) shows that the
meta-feature tree does not beat trivial baselines as a predictor
(hit rate 0.11 against 0.44 for the majority-class baseline, in
the 11-model study), and that the fixed policy ``foundation model
first, deviate on constraints'' is the lowest-regret one in the
descriptive evaluation. The resulting framework is deliberately
oriented to verifiable constraints (\emph{constraint-driven})
rather than learned routing --- a methodological position that
avoids overfitting recommenders to few observations.

Three observations close the chapter. First, none of the gaps is a
gap of formulation: the mechanics of distillation is classical
(Section~\ref{sec:rel-destilacao}), the benchmark protocols are
consolidated (Section~\ref{sec:rel-benchmarks}) --- the gaps are
ones of \emph{measurement under a uniform protocol}, exactly the
kind of contribution a neutral benchmark with reusable
infrastructure can make. Second, two of the gaps were partially
eroded during the project's execution --- \citet{poeta2024}
anticipated the independent KAN test in a narrow scope, and
\citet{jeong2026} reached point regression shortly before our delivery
--- and both erosions are recorded with dates and explicit
rescoping, rather than silenced; the distributional pillar and the
strategy frontier remained unclaimed up to the last recorded
search. Third, the table makes visible that the dissertation's
three empirical fronts (Chapters~\ref{chap:benchmark},
\ref{chap:framework} and~\ref{chap:destilacao}) attack gaps that
are distinct but chained: the benchmark generates the evidence the
decision framework organizes, and the cost inversion the benchmark
quantifies is precisely the problem the distillation attacks. With
the gaps mapped, Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia} specifies the
shared experimental protocol that attacks them, with the design's
limitations disclosed before any result.
