\chapter{The Decision Framework, Evaluated}
\label{chap:framework}

This chapter submits the decision framework, descriptive so far, to
evaluation through three studies on the benchmark's own datasets
(18; 17 wherever the FM family participates):
a negative result, in which meta-feature routing does not generalize
under leave-one-dataset-out validation; a positive result, in which
the fixed policy ``foundation model first'' is regret-optimal on the
primary metric; and a registered experiment on weighted
multi-criteria regret, which locates the latency weight at which that
recommendation flips --- shifting 10--20\% of the weight from
accuracy to latency (by the median; 20--30\% by the mean) is enough. The resulting framework is a flowchart
of four constraints verifiable \emph{a priori}, in which the first
question --- latency --- is now quantitatively justified.

\section{From descriptive to evaluated}
\label{sec:descritivo-validado}

The multi-criteria benchmark of Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}
produces, by construction, a practical artifact: given a new problem,
which model to use? The framework's first version answered with two
descriptive instruments. The first is the multi-criteria matrix,
which summarizes the position of each of the 14 models on six
criteria measured in the benchmark --- performance, training cost,
hyperparameter tuning cost, inference latency, robustness, and
interpretability. Figure~\ref{fig:decision-matrix} presents that
matrix, with scores of 1 to 3 stars computed from the benchmark (3 =
best; darker = better), and its reading motivates the rest of the
chapter.

\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{images/decision_matrix.png}
\caption[Multi-criteria decision matrix of the 14 models]{Multi-criteria

The matrix makes visible the central pattern of the comparison
between foundation models and GBDTs: what is optimal in performance
at the fine resolution of Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} is weak in
latency, and vice versa --- with the caveat that, at the matrix's own
resolution of thirds, XGBoost appears strong on every criterion (see
the caption). The second instrument, the constraint-oriented
flowchart (presented in its final form in
Section~\ref{sec:arcabouco-resultante}), encodes that trade-off as a
sequence of questions verifiable before any training.

Both instruments, however, are descriptive: they summarize what the
benchmark measured, but were never tested against datasets that
played no part in their construction. A recommendation framework
never exposed to that test is subject to the same overclaiming
critique that hits part of the algorithm-recommendation literature.
This chapter submits the framework to evaluation --- out of sample
for the learned component --- with two complementary results: a
negative one (what the framework should \emph{not} attempt) and a
positive, descriptive one (the policy the data support); then a
third, registered study extends the evaluation to the multi-criteria
scenario.

It is worth separating from the start what each study tests. The
out-of-sample test (LODO) applies to what is \emph{learned}: the
tree is trained on 17 datasets and evaluated on the eighteenth, and
it is that test it fails. The fixed policies learn nothing; their
regret describes what would have happened on the benchmark's 18
datasets, and the choice of ``always-FM'' as the best of them is
made on the same datasets on which it is measured. The flowchart of
Section~\ref{sec:arcabouco-resultante}, finally, is not evaluated as
a policy: it gathers what the three studies show, plus two
admissibility constraints (architectural limits and
interpretability) that none of the studies measures.

\section{The negative result: meta-feature routing does not generalize}
\label{sec:resultado-negativo}

The meta-learning literature, going back to the algorithm selection
problem \citep{rice1976}, suggests a seductive path: predicting the
winning algorithm from a dataset's meta-features --- size,
dimensionality, imbalance, fraction of categorical attributes. If it
worked, the decision framework would be a predictive model, not a
flowchart. We tested that promise with the honest protocol available
for $N{=}18$ datasets: leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) validation, in
which a depth-2 decision tree --- the same illustrative model of
Figure~\ref{fig:arvore-exemplo}, in
Chapter~\ref{chap:fundamentacao} --- is trained on the other 17
datasets to predict the winning family of the excluded one,
repeating the procedure for each of the 18.

The result contradicts the expectation created by the literature ---
which is why we report it with the protocol in plain view. In the
11-model study, the tree reaches a \textbf{hit rate of 0.11 against
0.44 for the majority-class baseline}; in the 14-model study
(including the 2026 generation), 0.72 against the baseline's 0.78.
In neither configuration does the learned router beat the trivial
rule ``always predict the family that wins most often''. The cause
is straightforward: with $N{=}18$ datasets,
each LODO round trains on 17 examples --- a sample in which any
partition by meta-features is dominated by the noise of individual
reversals, and in which the price of a wrong leaf is high. The
honest summary is this: learning meta-feature routing is not viable
under LODO with this model class at this scale of $N$ --- and
stating that under an explicit protocol protects the framework from
the overclaiming critique mentioned above. The tree remains in the
work as a \emph{descriptive illustration of the structure} of the
reversals between families, never as a predictor.

Independent, contemporary evidence points the same way: global
meta-feature approaches are not robust enough to explain or select
among models of the tabular foundation-model era \citep{herre2026}.
The convergence between our LODO protocol and an external study with
a different methodology reinforces that the negative result is not
an artifact of our dataset sample.

\emph{Meta-feature routing does not generalize under LODO.} With
$N{=}18$ datasets, a learned router (depth-2 tree) does not beat the
majority-class baseline in either model generation (0.11 vs.\ 0.44
with 11 models; 0.72 vs.\ 0.78 with 14). Nor does the result depend
on the router class: in a robustness experiment registered on
2026-10-14, a depth-3 tree, $k$NN ($k{=}3$, standardized
meta-features), and multinomial logistic regression, under the same
LODO protocol, also fail to beat the baseline in either grid --- 0
of 8 cells, including the depth-2 tree replica as a sanity check
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak aggregated/\allowbreak lodo\_\allowbreak routers.\allowbreak csv}).
The decision framework should not contain a winning-family predictor
--- a conclusion corroborated by independent evidence
\citep{herre2026}.

\section{The positive result: the ``FM first'' policy and its evolution}
\label{sec:resultado-positivo}

If learned routing does not work, the natural alternative is
\emph{fixed policies}: always recommending the same family,
whatever the dataset. To compare them, we measure what each policy
leaves on the table. On a given dataset, let $m^{*}$ be the primary
metric of the best of the 14 models, $m^{-}$ that of the worst, and
$m_{F}$ that of the best model of the family $F$ the policy
recommends. The policy's \textbf{normalized regret} on that dataset
is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{regret}(F) \;=\;
\frac{\lvert m^{*} - m_{F} \rvert}{\lvert m^{*} - m^{-} \rvert}.
\label{eq:regret-normalizado}
\end{equation}
It is 0 when the recommended family contains the dataset's best
model and 1 when the best it offers is the dataset's worst model;
0.10 means the recommendation cost one tenth of the distance between
best and worst. On \texttt{adult}, for example, the best model is a
foundation model: recommending the FM family has regret 0;
recommending GBDT, 0.10; recommending deep learning, 0.60. Two
reading notes. The policy is credited with the best model of the
recommended family --- it is a measure of the family's ceiling; the
single-model policies are reported below. And the normalization depends on
the dataset's worst model, so the regret compares policies within a
study, and the @11 and @14 columns of Table~\ref{tab:regret-lodo}
are not comparable line by line. Table~\ref{tab:regret-lodo}
summarizes the regret of the four policies --- the tree under LODO
and the three fixed policies --- in the two studies; the tree and
always-FM rows aggregate over the 17 datasets in which the FM family
participates (\texttt{helena} is excluded by architectural limit:
its 100 classes exceed the FMs' cap), and the always-GBDT and
always-DL rows over all 18.

Three readings emerge from the table. First, already in the original
study's 11-model core, ``use the foundation model by default'' was
the best fixed policy (mean regret 0.115; median 0.018). Second,
with the 2026 generation, the policy's median regret drops to
\textbf{zero}: on half or more of the datasets, the FM family
contains the best model outright. The question ``which family
wins?'' dissolves --- and with it the value of any performance
router. Third, the apparent tie between the tree and the always-FM
policy at the @14 median (both 0.000) deserves explanation, and the
explanation undoes it: with 14 models, the tree learns to predict
the FM family on nearly every dataset, collapsing into the fixed
policy --- while keeping a hit rate \emph{below} the majority-class
baseline (Section~\ref{sec:resultado-negativo}). The fixed policy
reaches the same regret without that failure mode; the tree's
collapse is one more symptom that there is no learnable structure,
not a victory of the router.

A fourth reading is mandatory: the ``Always-FM'' row picks, on each
dataset, the \emph{better} of the two foundation models by looking
at the outcome --- it is an \textbf{intra-family oracle}, measuring
the family's ceiling, not a policy a practitioner can execute. The
executable single-model policies, by the same yardstick:
\textbf{always-TabFM} has mean regret 0.080, median 0.000, and worst
case \textbf{1.000} (\texttt{telco\_customer\_churn}, where TabFM is
last of 14 on the primary metric --- a last place sensitive to
preprocessing: with the \texttt{TotalCharges} column numeric, TabFM
rises to the dataset's best AUC among the 14 re-evaluated models,
Chapter~\ref{chap:metodologia}); \textbf{always-TabPFN}, mean 0.261.
The zero median therefore survives in the executable ``TabFM by
default'' policy --- but the 0.080 mean and the 1.000 worst case are
its own, which is why the framework's practical recommendation is
never ``use the FM blindly'' but ``FM first, with the cheap boundary
checks'' (Section~\ref{sec:arcabouco-resultante}).

\emph{``Foundation model first'' is the regret-optimal policy on the
primary metric.} In the 2026 generation, the FM family's ceiling
reaches mean regret 0.021 and median 0.000 (descriptive, over the 17
datasets in which the family participates) --- no learned or fixed
policy beats that ceiling, and the executable always-TabFM preserves
the zero median (by the mean, its 0.080 sits above even the LODO
tree's 0.051 --- a tree that nonetheless fails as a predictor,
Section~\ref{sec:resultado-negativo}). The choice of \emph{family} by the primary metric no longer
depends on the dataset --- the choice of \emph{model} within it
still does (the model$\times$dataset interaction dominates the rank
variance in Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}); the caveat is that this
result holds for the primary metric in isolation, a question the
next section confronts.

\section{Weighted multi-criteria regret: when the recommendation flips}
\label{sec:regret-ponderado}

The positive result of the previous section has a declared
limitation: the regret is computed on the primary metric, not on
multi-criteria combinations weighted by user preferences. The
limitation is not cosmetic. Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} showed that
the accuracy leader costs 4--5 orders of magnitude more to serve
than a GBDT; a practitioner whose utility function weighs accuracy
\emph{and} inference cost may legitimately reach another
recommendation. This section closes that gap with experiment E2,
which asks: does the ``FM first'' policy remain regret-optimal when
the decision metric weighs accuracy against cost? The design (weight
grid, policies, and metric) was written before the result was read,
but the hypothesis record already carries the result --- no
independent timestamp proves that the design preceded the result ---
so the correct label is \emph{registered analysis}, not
pre-registration.

\subsection{Registered design}
\label{sec:e2-desenho}

The design defines a multi-criteria score per model$\times$dataset:
\begin{equation}
S \;=\; w \cdot r_{\text{acc}} \;+\; (1-w) \cdot r_{\text{lat}},
\label{eq:score-ponderado}
\end{equation}
where $r_{\text{acc}}$ is the model's rank on the dataset's primary
metric normalized to $[0,1]$ and $r_{\text{lat}}$ is the normalized
rank of inference latency --- both oriented so that \textbf{0 is the
best} model of the dataset and 1 the worst, so the best score $S$ is
the \emph{lowest} --- and
$w \in \{1.0,\, 0.9,\, \ldots,\, 0.0\}$ sweeps the preference
spectrum --- from ``only accuracy matters'' ($w{=}1$) to ``only
latency matters'' ($w{=}0$). An example shows how the weight acts
and why the crossover comes early. On a dataset where a foundation
model is the most accurate ($r_{\text{acc}}{=}0$) and the slowest
($r_{\text{lat}}{=}1$), its score is $S = 1-w$; a GBDT that is the
fastest ($r_{\text{lat}}{=}0$) and fourth in accuracy out of 14
($r_{\text{acc}} \approx 0.23$) has $S = 0.23\,w$. The two tie at
$w \approx 0.81$: with less than ${\sim}80\%$ of the weight on
accuracy, the GBDT moves ahead. The rank normalization ignores
whether the slowest model is twice as slow or a hundred thousand
times slower
--- whoever is last in latency carries the whole $(1-w)$ term ---
and, since the accuracy leader (TabFM) is the slowest of the grid
and TabPFN is among the three slowest, the crossover point is
governed by the distance, in accuracy ranks, between them and the
fast models, which is small. The choice of ranks is deliberate (the
latencies differ by five orders of magnitude, and a cardinal scale
would be dominated by them), but it means that ``10--20\% of the
weight'' is a claim about ranks, not about microseconds. For each
value of $w$, the normalized regret of each fixed policy is computed
exactly as in the original study: 0 if the recommended family
contains the model with the lowest score $S$ on the dataset; 1 if it
contains only the worst.\footnote{The LODO tree is evaluated by the
original study's per-dataset recommendation, without re-fitting. The
result reiterates Section~\ref{sec:resultado-negativo}: in median,
the tree never beats the best fixed policy at any $w$, and collapses
into the always-FM policy at high weights
(\texttt{results/\allowbreak aggregated/\allowbreak lodo\_\allowbreak weighted\_\allowbreak regret.\allowbreak csv}).}
The $N$s mirror those of Table~\ref{tab:regret-lodo}: 18 datasets
for the always-GBDT and always-DL policies, 17 for always-FM
(\texttt{helena} excluded by architectural limit). The registered
hypothesis H-W1 states: there exists $w^{*} < 1$ from which
``always-GBDT'' has a lower median regret than ``always-FM'' ---
which would make the flowchart (question 1 = latency) the correct
form of the policy, and not a cosmetic refinement.

One design limitation was declared in the record itself and must be
repeated here: latency uses as a \emph{proxy} the standardized
measurement on \texttt{adult} --- the only one available for the 14
models under an identical protocol. Relative latency between models
is approximately stable across datasets for GBDTs and deep-learning
models; for foundation models, however, it grows with the context
size, so the proxy is conservative in the FMs' favor on small
datasets and against them on large ones. The caveat was later closed
with measurements at the benchmark's two size extremes; the test is
at the end of Section~\ref{sec:e2-resultados}.

The joint reading of the figure and the table establishes three
facts. First, \textbf{hypothesis H-W1 is confirmed, and the
crossover is early}: on the sweep grid, the always-FM policy keeps a
zero median regret only at $w \in \{0.9,\, 1.0\}$. Already at
$w^{*}{=}0.8$ --- just 20\% of the weight on latency --- both
always-GBDT (0.067) and always-DL (0.035) have a lower median regret
than always-FM (0.096); the crossover therefore occurs between
$w{=}0.9$ and $w{=}0.8$. By the mean (right panel of
Figure~\ref{fig:lodo-weighted}), the picture is slightly more
favorable to the FMs: always-FM is still the best policy at
$w{=}0.8$ (0.119 against the GBDT's 0.143 and the DL's 0.146) and
only loses at $w{=}0.7$ (0.243 against the GBDT's 0.105). Second, in
the intermediate band the best fixed policy by the median is
\emph{always-DL} ($0.035$ at $w{=}0.8$; $0.000$ at $w{=}0.7$), and
from $w \leq 0.6$ on, ``always-GBDT'' has a median regret of exactly
zero --- as soon as latency dominates the utility function, the GBDT
family contains the best model on half or more of the datasets,
mirroring at the opposite extreme what the FM family does at
$w{=}1$. Third, at the extreme $w{=}0$ (latency only), always-FM's
median regret reaches 0.846: the family that contains the best model
in accuracy contains nearly the worst in inference cost, the
numerical picture of the trade-off that
Figure~\ref{fig:decision-matrix} showed in stars.\footnote{At
$w{=}1.0$, the score of Eq.~\ref{eq:score-ponderado} reduces to the
primary-metric rank; the values differ marginally from those of
Table~\ref{tab:regret-lodo} (always-FM mean regret 0.023 here
against 0.021 there) because the original study's regret is
normalized on the primary-metric scale and E2's on the rank scale.
The medians coincide at 0.000.}

The interpretation pre-specified in the experiment's record applies
directly: whatever $w^{*}$ turned out to be, the result would enrich
the framework by quantifying the trade-off it already encoded
qualitatively. The value found --- a crossover between $w{=}0.9$ and
$w{=}0.8$ by the median --- does more than enrich: it explains
\emph{why the flowchart's first question is latency}. If the
recommendation only held with 99\% of the weight on accuracy, the
latency question would be an edge detail; since it flips with
10--20\% of the weight on latency (by the median), the question is
the axis of the decision. The flowchart of the next section is not a decorative
heuristic around a single policy --- it is the encoding of the
frontier that Figure~\ref{fig:lodo-weighted} measures. Put another
way: before E2, we knew latency \emph{mattered} (the matrix of
Figure~\ref{fig:decision-matrix} already showed it in stars); after
E2, we know \emph{how much} it needs to matter to change the answer
--- and the answer is: little.

\emph{The ``FM first'' recommendation flips with 10--20\% of the
weight on latency, by median regret.} Under the weighted score
$S = w \cdot r_{\text{acc}} + (1-w) \cdot r_{\text{lat}}$, the
always-FM policy is regret-optimal in median only for $w \geq 0.9$;
at $w{=}0.8$ it is already beaten by always-GBDT and always-DL, and
for $w \leq 0.6$ ``always-GBDT'' has a zero median regret. The proxy
caveat accompanies the finding --- and was tested: re-running the
sweep with the latency rankings measured on \texttt{credit\_g} (a
thousand samples) and
\texttt{cardiovascular\_\allowbreak disease} (70{,}000) in place of
\texttt{adult}, the crossover is identical in all three cases
(always-FM regret-optimal only at $w \geq 0.9$); only the extent of
the zone in which always-GBDT has a zero median shifts by
$\pm 0.1$ ($w \leq 0.7$ with the small dataset's proxy, $w \leq 0.5$
with the large one's), exactly in the bias direction declared in the
record. The flip of the recommendation with 10--20\% of the weight
on latency (by the median) therefore does not depend on the dataset
on which latency was measured. A second dependency is declared: the band is measured
under the design's \textbf{rank normalization} --- deliberately
chosen for robustness to the five orders of magnitude separating the
latencies --- and the specific 10--20\% band is a property of that
design: alternative normalizations (logarithmic, linear) could shift
the crossover point; no versioned artifact measures them, so the
framework relies only on the rank-based reading established above.

\section{The resulting framework: decision by constraints}
\label{sec:arcabouco-resultante}

The three studies converge on the same conclusion: what remains
informative in the decision --- and what the flowchart always
encoded --- are \textbf{constraints verifiable \emph{a priori}}, not
performance predictions. Figure~\ref{fig:flowchart-14} presents the
flowchart in its final form, with the full study's 14 models.

\begin{figure}[htb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth]{images/decision_flowchart_14_en.pdf}
\caption[Decision flowchart]{Decision flowchart, 14-model study: each
diamond is a constraint verifiable \emph{a priori}.}
\label{fig:flowchart-14}
\end{figure}

The recommendation fits in four questions, asked in this order
before training any model: must predictions come out in microseconds
per row? does the problem have more than 10 classes, or more rows
than the foundation model accepts as context? does the domain demand
a model interpretable by construction? --- if any answer is yes, the
indicated family is the one the constraint leaves standing; if none
is, the default is the foundation model, compared against a tuned
GBDT before adoption, because its measured worst case is last place
on one dataset. The four constraints, in the order the flowchart
checks them:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Latency and inference cost.} The accuracy leader
    costs 4--5 orders of magnitude more to serve
    (Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}); if the latency budget is in
    microseconds, the FM family is excluded in its native form.
    Experiment E2 quantifies why this is the first question: the
    recommendation flips with just 10--20\% of the weight on latency
    (by the median; 20--30\% by the mean;
    Section~\ref{sec:regret-ponderado}).
    Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao} measures one route toward that
    budget --- distilling TabPFN's predictive distribution in
    regression, with the student serving at hundreds of microseconds
    per row --- with a positive result delimited to that framing.
  \item \textbf{Architectural limits.} More than 10 classes (TabFM's
    limit) or overflowing the context limit are objective structural
    exclusions --- the \texttt{helena} case (100 classes) in the
    benchmark itself.
  \item \textbf{Intrinsic interpretability required.} When the
    domain's regulation demands intrinsically interpretable models,
    the recommendation is the GBDTs --- the family whose
    attribution-based explanations are commonly accepted in
    regulated practice, and the only one with a simultaneously
    strong latency profile in the matrix of
    Figure~\ref{fig:decision-matrix}; a boosted ensemble of
    hundreds of trees is not intrinsically interpretable in the
    strict sense, and domains demanding that should look at shallow
    trees or additive models, outside this benchmark.
  \item \textbf{No active constraint.} The regret-optimal default
    applies: foundation model, with TabPFN \citep{hollmann2025} as
    the mature option and TabFM \citep{tabfm2026} as the frontier,
    with the maturity caveats discussed in
    Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark}.
\end{enumerate}

The three studies are consistent with these four questions capturing
the essential: the constraints are the decision's \emph{actual
informative content}, and raw performance has stopped discriminating
within the leading family --- though the flowchart itself, as stated
above, is a synthesis of the three studies, not an evaluated
policy. The resulting framework is deliberately
modest in form --- a flowchart, not a meta-model --- because that is
the form the data support.

\section{Limitations of the framework}
\label{sec:limitacoes-framework}

This chapter's limitations are declared in the same currency as its
results.

As for the sample's composition, the dataset-identity erratum
(Table~\ref{tab:datasets}) does not alter this chapter's readings:
the binarized \texttt{california\_\allowbreak housing} participates
in LODO and E2 as an internally consistent task (all 14 models
compete on the same target, and the ranks used by the scores are
internal to each dataset), and \texttt{online\_news} only had its
label corrected. As for the dataset sample size, $N{=}18$ precludes
fine statistical validation of the framework itself; LODO is the
honest tool possible at that scale, and that is exactly why the
negative result of Section~\ref{sec:resultado-negativo} is phrased
as ``not learnable under LODO with this model class'', not as a
general impossibility.

As for the metric, the original study computes the regret on the
primary metric. The multi-criteria extension of
Section~\ref{sec:regret-ponderado} closes part of that gap, but
inherits two simplifications of its own: the score weighs only two
criteria (accuracy and latency) of a utility function that, in
practice, may include training cost, robustness, and
interpretability; and latency enters through a proxy measured on a
single declared machine. The sensitivity to the measurement dataset,
however, is no longer an unknown: the sweep re-run with the proxies
from the benchmark's size extremes produces the same crossover
(Section~\ref{sec:e2-resultados}), leaving as limitations only the
single machine and the two criteria.

Finally, the dating: the model frontier changes in cycles of months
--- the difference between the @11 and @14 columns of
Table~\ref{tab:regret-lodo} is itself the demonstration. The
framework is dated by construction and versioned with the benchmark
that supports it; recommendations derived from it should cite the
study version (11 or 14 models) they refer to.

\section{Chapter summary}

This chapter submitted the descriptive instruments of
Chapter~\ref{chap:benchmark} to three evaluation studies --- the
only learned component, the tree, under LODO: learned meta-feature
routing does not generalize (hit rate 0.11 vs.\ 0.44 with 11 models;
0.72 vs.\ 0.78 with 14); the fixed policy ``foundation model first''
is regret-optimal on the primary metric (median regret 0.000 in the
2026 generation, preserved in the executable always-TabFM policy,
whose mean is 0.080 and whose worst case is 1.000); and the weighted
multi-criteria regret, registered experiment E2, locates the point
where that recommendation flips --- between $w{=}0.9$ and $w{=}0.8$
of accuracy weight by the median --- and thereby quantitatively
justifies the flowchart's architecture: latency is the first
question because it is the one that changes the answer earliest. The
obstacle remaining between the practitioner and the regret-optimal
policy --- inference latency --- motivates
Chapter~\ref{chap:destilacao}, which measures one specific route to
reduce it: distilling the TabPFN teacher's predictive distribution
into a gradient-boosting student, in regression --- one framing, not
the general removal of the obstacle.
